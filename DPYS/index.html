Overview

Caution — Indirect Evidence Only: The AD-relevant signal for DPYS derives from a single mouse toxicant model (1,3,6,8-tetrabromocarbazole). No DPYS changes have been demonstrated in human AD brain tissue, and no targeted experiments directly link DPYS manipulation to Aβ, p-Tau, or cognition. Additionally, DPYS loss-of-function is associated with neurological disease and fluoropyrimidine toxicity; indiscriminate suppression may be harmful.

DPYS (dihydropyrimidinase/DHPase) is primarily a pyrimidine-catabolism enzyme linked to β-alanine metabolism, fluoropyrimidine drug metabolism, and rare neurometabolic disease. Its strongest Alzheimer's disease–relevant evidence comes from a 2026 mouse study of 1,3,6,8-tetrabromocarbazole (1368-BCZ), where subchronic exposure produced an Alzheimer's-like phenotype: impaired recognition memory, spatial learning and memory deficits, hippocampal injury, approximately 3-fold increased hippocampal Aβ, and approximately 1.5–2-fold increased phosphorylated Tau.

In that model, hippocampal Dpys was downregulated alongside Mlycd and Pank1, and multi-omics analysis connected these changes to disruption of β-alanine metabolism and pantothenate/CoA biosynthesis — creating a plausible mechanistic link between reduced DPYS activity, metabolic instability in the hippocampus, and AD-like pathology.

However, the evidence remains indirect. The text does not establish DPYS as a validated Alzheimer's target in humans, does not show DPYS changes in AD patient brain tissue, and does not report targeted experiments proving that manipulating Dpys alters Aβ, p-Tau, cognition, or neurodegeneration. As an Alzheimer's therapeutic target, DPYS is best considered an exploratory metabolic-modulation candidate.

Key Insights

Metabolic Intersection

DPYS sits at the junction of pyrimidine degradation, β-alanine production, and broader cofactor/energy metabolism — making it a pathway node rather than a single-function enzyme. Disruption of this hub ramifies into mitochondrial energy handling, lipid metabolism, and acetylation capacity, all relevant to neurodegeneration.

AD-Relevant Signal (Mouse)

The 1368-BCZ mouse study provides the most direct AD-relevant evidence: Dpys was downregulated in hippocampus concurrent with impaired memory, elevated Aβ (~3×) and p-Tau (~1.5–2×), hippocampal pathology, and disruption of the β-alanine and pantothenate/CoA axes. The signal is real but indirect.

β-alanine–CoA Axis as Mechanism

The most plausible DPYS-related AD mechanism runs through the β-alanine–pantothenate–CoA axis. CoA metabolism shapes mitochondrial energy flux, lipid biosynthesis, and protein acetylation — all of which affect neuronal resilience. This axis represents the most tractable mechanistic hypothesis for follow-up investigation.

Safety: Loss-of-Function Risks

Biallelic DPYS variants cause dihydropyrimidinase deficiency with variable neurological phenotypes: developmental delay, seizures, autistic traits, hypotonia, brain atrophy, white matter abnormalities, and juvenile parkinsonism. Furthermore, reduced DPYS activity increases fluoropyrimidine/5-FU toxicity — a critical pharmacogenomic safety concern in older populations who may also receive cancer chemotherapy.

Broader Metabolic Collapse Context

DPYS downregulation in the AD-like toxicant model appears to be part of a wider metabolic disruption (hundreds of altered hippocampal metabolites) rather than an isolated change. This context-dependency complicates direct causal attribution and underscores the need for targeted perturbation experiments.

Exploratory Target Status

Despite CNS relevance from human deficiency data, pharmacogenomic importance, and appearance in neuroinflammatory and hippocampal metabolic studies, DPYS has not been validated as an Alzheimer's target in human brain tissue. No AD-specific DPYS methylation, expression, or variant data from patient cohorts were identified. DPYS should be treated as an exploratory metabolic-modulation candidate and biomarker hypothesis, not an established disease-modifying target.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Prioritize validation before therapeutic development: Test whether Dpys rescue or knockdown specifically changes Aβ accumulation, Tau phosphorylation, hippocampal pathology, synaptic function, and cognition in AD-relevant models before advancing any therapeutic program.
  2. Examine DPYS in human AD datasets: Systematically assess DPYS expression, methylation, variants, and linked metabolite levels across hippocampus and other vulnerable brain regions in AD patient cohorts, stratifying by disease stage or subtype.
  3. Investigate the β-alanine–pantothenate–CoA axis: Focus mechanistic studies on this axis as the most plausible DPYS-related AD pathway, especially regarding effects on mitochondrial function, lipid metabolism, acetylation balance, and neuronal resilience.
  4. Deploy metabolomics in AD-relevant contexts: Measure DPYS-related metabolites — dihydrouracil, dihydrothymine, β-alanine products, pantothenate, and CoA-pathway intermediates — in AD model systems, CSF, plasma, or post-mortem brain tissue to establish whether this metabolic signature tracks with disease.
  5. Avoid assuming DPYS inhibition is beneficial: Known DPYS loss-of-function causes neurological disease and increases fluoropyrimidine toxicity; indiscriminate suppression of DPYS activity may be harmful and should not be pursued without robust mechanistic justification.
  6. Target metabolic flux normalization, not simple modulation: Any therapeutic strategy should aim to normalize DPYS-linked metabolic flux or compensate for downstream pathway disruption rather than simply activating or inhibiting the enzyme in isolation.
  7. Incorporate pharmacogenetic safety screening: Include DPYS variant screening in any DPYS-directed therapeutic or biomarker strategy, particularly because DPYS variants influence fluoropyrimidine toxicity risk and older AD patients may concurrently undergo cancer treatment.
  8. Treat DPYS as a candidate biomarker for metabolic/inflammatory AD subtypes: Until causality is demonstrated, position DPYS as a pathway node and biomarker hypothesis for metabolic or neuroinflammatory AD subpopulations rather than a primary disease-modifying target.

Overall Assessment

Synthesis & Verdict

Overall Assessment — DPYS represents a biologically plausible but insufficiently validated candidate in the Alzheimer's disease target landscape. Its most compelling AD-relevant evidence is restricted to a single toxicant mouse model (1368-BCZ), where hippocampal Dpys downregulation co-occurred with AD-like pathology and disruption of the β-alanine–pantothenate–CoA metabolic axis. Human genetic data confirm that DPYS is important for CNS integrity, but human AD-specific evidence is entirely absent from the current literature. Critically, DPYS loss-of-function is itself pathogenic — causing neurological disease and fluoropyrimidine toxicity — meaning that therapeutic strategies must be designed with great care to avoid mimicking a loss-of-function state. The immediate research priority should be direct causal validation through targeted perturbation studies in AD models, combined with systematic interrogation of DPYS and its metabolic network in human AD brain and biofluid datasets. Until causality is established, DPYS is best positioned as an exploratory metabolic pathway node and biomarker hypothesis for metabolic or neuroinflammatory AD subtypes, not as a lead therapeutic target.
