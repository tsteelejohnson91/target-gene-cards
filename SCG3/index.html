Overview

Caveat — Therapeutic Uncertainty: SCG3 is substantially downregulated in AD brain and CSF, meaning simple neutralization strategies borrowed from retinal-disease models may be inappropriate. No direct AD therapeutic evidence currently exists; AD-specific target validation is required before drug development.

SCG3 (secretogranin III) is best supported by the current evidence as an Alzheimer's disease–associated biomarker and mechanistic clue, not as a validated AD therapeutic target. The strongest AD-specific evidence comes from 2025 studies showing that SCG3 is substantially downregulated in AD, has strong diagnostic performance, is reduced across multiple single-cell populations, and is significantly decreased in the hippocampus. A separate ADNI CSF proteomics/MRI study independently identified SCG3 among five proteins consistently associated with AD-related brain imaging traits.

Mechanistically, SCG3 fits into neurosecretory/granin-family biology involving regulated secretion, dense-core vesicles, neuropeptide signaling, synaptic function, cognition, and memory. Its repeated co-occurrence with VGF, SCG2, CHGB, APP, APOE, NCAN, and other synaptic or secretory proteins suggests that SCG3 may reflect disruption of neuronal secretory, synaptic, and neuropeptide-processing pathways in AD and related dementias.

SCG3 also has indirect AD relevance through cerebral amyloid angiopathy, neurovascular dysfunction, inflammation, metabolic disease, astrocyte activation, and pathological angiogenesis. Outside AD, SCG3 is repeatedly described as a disease-selective angiogenic and vascular leakage factor — especially in diabetic retinopathy and choroidal neovascularization — where anti-SCG3 antibodies show therapeutic efficacy, demonstrating druggability without establishing that SCG3 inhibition would benefit AD.

Key Insights

Diagnostic Performance

SCG3 was selected by LASSO, SVM-RFE, and random forest as one of six core AD-associated genes, with diagnostic AUC values of 0.845, 0.927, and 0.917 across training and validation datasets (Fan et al. 2025). Downregulation was confirmed across bulk transcriptomics, single-cell populations, and hippocampal animal models.

CSF Proteomics & Brain Structure

The ADNI CSF proteomics/MRI study independently identified SCG3 alongside APP, VGF, APOE, and NCAN as proteins consistently associated with AD-related imaging-derived brain traits. This suggests SCG3 may be linked to structural brain degeneration, not merely nonspecific expression change.

Granin Biology & Synaptic Network

SCG3 belongs to the granin family — neurosecretory proteins involved in peptide neurotransmission, cognition, memory, stress, energy balance, and inflammation. Its repeated co-occurrence with VGF, SCG2, CHGB, NPTX1, and NRXN2 points to a disrupted neurosecretory/synaptic program in AD and related dementias.

CAA & Vascular Pathology Link

In sporadic cerebral amyloid angiopathy, CSF SCG3 was decreased together with SCG1, SCG2, SCG5, VGF, NPTX1, and NRXN2 — linking SCG3 to amyloid-β vascular pathology, synaptic protein loss, and neurosecretory dysfunction relevant to both AD and vascular cognitive impairment.

Ocular Druggability Evidence

Anti-SCG3 antibodies and AAV-delivered anti-SCG3 Fab fragments reduced vascular leakage, pathological neovascularization, leukocyte infiltration, macrophage activation, and choroidal neovascularization — often with VEGF-independent, disease-selective effects. These data establish SCG3 as a druggable target, though not yet in an AD context.

Compartment-Specific Interpretation Required

Plasma SCG3 showed negative genetic associations with intelligence and cognitive performance in Mendelian randomization. This need not contradict brain SCG3 downregulation, since circulating SCG3 may reflect peripheral vascular, endocrine, or metabolic processes rather than neuronal expression. Therapeutic direction depends critically on compartment and tissue context.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Prioritize SCG3 as a candidate AD biomarker in studies integrating transcriptomics, CSF proteomics, single-cell data, hippocampal pathology, and MRI-derived structural brain traits — not as an immediate drug target.
  2. Validate compartment-specific relationships by comparing CSF, plasma, and brain SCG3 across AD stages, amyloid burden, tau pathology, hippocampal atrophy, synaptic loss, cognitive decline, and vascular comorbidity to determine whether signals are consistent or divergent.
  3. Treat SCG3 as a compartment-dependent target: brain SCG3 loss may suggest restoration or preservation of neurosecretory function, while vascular or peripheral SCG3 elevation — if demonstrated in AD — could support inhibition only in selected, well-defined contexts.
  4. Do not assume ocular anti-SCG3 strategies translate to AD. AD-specific models must characterize whether SCG3 is elevated, sequestered, depleted, or mislocalized in neurons, astrocytes, microglia, endothelial cells, pericytes, or cerebral vessels before therapeutic design.
  5. Test SCG3 modulation on AD-relevant outcomes including amyloid-β processing/clearance, tau phosphorylation, synaptic vesicle function, neuropeptide secretion, hippocampal plasticity, neuroinflammation, blood–brain barrier integrity, vascular leakage, and cognition.
  6. Investigate SCG3 within its protein network — alongside VGF, SCG2, CHGB, APP, APOE, NCAN, NPTX1, NRXN2, and BACE1 — to determine whether it participates in a broader neurosecretory signature of AD progression.
  7. Explore SCG3 in mixed AD/vascular and CAA contexts, where amyloid vascular deposition, BBB dysfunction, inflammatory protease changes, and synaptic/granin protein decreases may make SCG3 especially informative for stratification.
  8. Account for metabolic and inflammatory confounders including obesity, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, oxidative stress, AGEs/RAGE signaling, and macrophage activation, all of which may influence SCG3 biology and also modify AD risk.
  9. Reserve therapeutic development until direct AD evidence defines whether SCG3 should be restored, stabilized, inhibited, or used only for patient stratification and disease monitoring.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — SCG3 in Alzheimer's Disease

Current evidence positions SCG3 as a promising, multi-level AD biomarker with genuine mechanistic relevance but no established therapeutic role in Alzheimer's disease. Its consistent downregulation across bulk transcriptomics, single-cell populations, hippocampal tissue, and CSF proteomics — alongside independent ADNI imaging associations — argues that SCG3 reflects disrupted neurosecretory, synaptic, and dense-core vesicle programs central to AD pathophysiology. Its co-occurrence with VGF, SCG2, CHGB, NPTX1, and NRXN2 situates it within a coherent granin/synaptic secretory signature of neurodegeneration. The strong preclinical druggability data from ocular vascular disease confirm that SCG3 is a tractable molecular target, but the therapeutic direction in AD is unresolved: because brain and CSF SCG3 are reduced rather than elevated in AD, neutralization strategies cannot be assumed safe or beneficial without direct AD-specific evidence. SCG3 should be advanced as a biomarker priority and a hypothesis-generating neurovascular/neurosecretory node, with therapeutic development contingent on rigorous compartment-specific target validation in AD-relevant models.
