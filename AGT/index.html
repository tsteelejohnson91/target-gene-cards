AGT / Angiotensinogen & Alzheimer's Disease

Target Enabling Package — AI-Assisted Evidence Summary

Overview

⚠ Interpretive Caution: The evidence connecting AGT directly to Alzheimer's disease is mostly indirect. AGT is a well-validated RAS/RAAS node for hypertension, vascular disease, and metabolic injury, but direct evidence linking AGT to amyloid-β, tau pathology, or clinical dementia outcomes is absent from this corpus. Therapeutic translation requires AD-specific validation.

AGT (angiotensinogen) is the upstream precursor of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAS/RAAS), supplying the substrate for downstream angiotensin peptides, most critically angiotensin II. Across the supplied evidence, AGT is repeatedly linked to hypertension, vascular dysfunction, stroke risk, oxidative stress, inflammation, endothelial and blood–brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction, metabolic disease, kidney injury, fibrosis, senescence, mitochondrial stress, and tissue remodeling.

The strongest Alzheimer's-relevant signals include: senescent astrocytes upregulating AGT and losing the ability to support BBB integrity (with AGT knockdown restoring this capacity); salt-sensitive hypertension inducing Alzheimer-like pathology through astrocytic Agt upregulation, neuronal AT1 receptor signaling, increased phosphorylated tau, reduced acetylcholine, dendritic spine loss, and cognitive impairment; and an ICV-STZ Alzheimer's mouse proteomics study placing AGT within networks involving mitochondrial dysfunction, synaptic disruption, neuroinflammation, and calcium/glutamate signaling changes.

AGT is best interpreted from this corpus as a plausible upstream modifier of Alzheimer's risk or progression through vascular, inflammatory, metabolic, and neurovascular mechanisms — not as a validated amyloid- or tau-specific Alzheimer's target.

Mechanistic Overview

Panel A (Upstream RAS): AGT is cleaved by renin to produce Ang I, processed by ACE or ACE2 into divergent peptides. Panel B (Harmful Axis): ACE → Ang II → AT1R drives two parallel cascades — vasoconstriction/BBB dysfunction and inflammation/neuronal AT1R signaling — converging on cognitive impairment with elevated phospho-tau and dendritic spine loss. Panel C (Protective Axis): ACE2 → Ang-(1–7) → Mas receptor counteracts AT1R, supporting vascular protection and BBB integrity. Panel D (Neurovascular Unit): Senescent astrocytes upregulate AGT and impair BBB (knockdown restores barrier); salt-sensitive hypertension drives astrocytic Agt upregulation, reduced GluT1, elevated pTau, reduced acetylcholine, and dendritic spine loss — amenable to RAS-targeted therapeutics.

Key Insights

Central RAS Node

AGT is the master upstream substrate of the RAS/RAAS. Its cleavage initiates the cascade producing angiotensin II, which activates AT1R-linked vasoconstriction, oxidative stress, inflammation, and tissue injury. The ACE/Ang II/AT1R axis is contrasted with the protective ACE2/Ang-(1–7)/Mas axis throughout the evidence base.

Astrocyte AGT & BBB Dysfunction

Senescent human astrocytes show markedly increased AGT mRNA and protein. In astrocyte–brain endothelial coculture models, this impairs endothelial barrier support. Crucially, AGT knockdown restored the barrier-promoting effect, providing the strongest direct neurovascular evidence for a causal role of excess astrocytic AGT in aging-related BBB breakdown.

Salt-HTN → Alzheimer-Like Pathology

Salt-sensitive hypertension induces a mechanistic chain: astrocytic Agt upregulation, neuronal Agtr1a signaling, reduced endothelial GluT1, mitochondrial stress, increased phosphorylated tau, reduced acetylcholine, dendritic spine loss, and cognitive impairment. Neuronal AT1a receptor knockout prevented several of these phenotypes, anchoring AGT/RAS as mechanistically upstream.

Vascular Risk & Stroke

Vascular relevance is the most consistent Alzheimer's-adjacent theme. A human MESA analysis found higher circulating angiotensinogen associated with increased ischemic stroke risk — particularly in men and independent of blood pressure — suggesting AGT contributes to vascular brain injury beyond simple hypertension. Hypertension, salt sensitivity, microvascular injury, and diabetes complications are major contributors to mixed Alzheimer's/vascular dementia.

Genetic Variation & Population Specificity

AGT variants including M235T/rs699, rs5050, rs5051, rs4762, and rs5046 are studied across hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and diabetic complications. Findings are population-specific and inconsistent. AGT genotypes should be treated as vascular risk modifiers, not universal Alzheimer's biomarkers.

Therapeutic Tractability (Outside AD)

Therapeutic targeting of AGT is highly advanced in cardiovascular contexts. Zilebesiran, a GalNAc-conjugated siRNA, achieves >90% hepatic AGT suppression with sustained blood-pressure reduction lasting months. ARBs, ACE inhibitors, renin inhibitors, antisense oligonucleotides, and ACE2/Ang-(1–7)/Mas enhancers are additional strategies. Alzheimer's-specific CNS efficacy, however, remains undemonstrated.

RAS Balance Over AGT Alone

Multiple studies show benefit from shifting signaling away from AT1R toward ACE2/Ang-(1–7)/Mas. AGT should not be viewed in isolation — the full RAS axis (renin, ACE, ACE2, Ang II, Ang-(1–7), AT1R, AT2R, Mas) must be assessed together. Context-dependent effects caution against assuming universal AGT inhibition is desirable: in some settings reduced AGT is associated with olfactory dysfunction or loss of Agt-labeled astrocytes in neurodegeneration.

Confounded Literature — Non-AGT Meanings

Several "AGT" mentions in the corpus are unrelated to angiotensinogen: alanine:glyoxylate aminotransferase (primary hyperoxaluria), O6-alkylguanine-DNA alkyltransferase/MGMT, adaptive graph Transformer, agarotetrol, abnormal glucose tolerance, plant enzymes, author initials, and device abbreviations. These must be excluded from any Alzheimer's evidence synthesis.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Treat AGT as hypothesis-generating, not validated. Biological plausibility is strong through vascular, inflammatory, oxidative, metabolic, and neurovascular pathways, but direct evidence of AGT modifying amyloid, tau, synaptic loss, or clinical cognition in Alzheimer's disease is absent from this corpus.
  2. Prioritize high-vascular-risk contexts. AGT/RAS is most likely to matter in patients or models with hypertension, salt sensitivity, stroke history, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, metabolic syndrome, vascular inflammation, BBB dysfunction, or mixed Alzheimer's/vascular cognitive impairment.
  3. Test AGT in Alzheimer's-specific cellular systems. Needed studies include AGT expression profiling across Alzheimer's brain regions and single-cell populations (astrocytes, endothelial cells, pericytes, neurons, microglia), and association with CSF/plasma amyloid, tau, neurodegeneration, and vascular biomarkers.
  4. Focus mechanistic work on the neurovascular unit. The strongest Alzheimer's-relevant evidence points to astrocyte-derived AGT impairing endothelial barrier support during senescence, and to hypertension-driven astrocyte–neuron RAS signaling affecting tau phosphorylation, acetylcholine, dendritic spines, and cognition.
  5. Evaluate full RAS balance, not AGT alone. Measure AGT together with renin, ACE, ACE2, Ang II, Ang-(1–7), AT1R/AGTR1, AT2R, Mas receptor, oxidative-stress markers, inflammatory cytokines, and BBB/endothelial readouts to capture the axis shift, not a single node.
  6. Favor protective RAS rebalancing over broad AGT suppression. Candidate strategies include ARBs, ACE inhibitors, renin inhibitors, ACE2 activators, Ang-(1–7)/Mas pathway enhancement, and selected AGT-lowering RNA therapies in well-defined high-risk populations.
  7. Use AGT-targeted RNA therapies cautiously in older adults. Zilebesiran and related AGT-silencing approaches demonstrate strong antihypertensive potential, but no CNS target engagement, cognitive benefit, or amyloid/tau effects are shown. Long-term monitoring for hypotension, renal dysfunction, electrolyte imbalance, impaired adaptive RAS responses, and cerebral hypoperfusion is required.
  8. Explore AGT within a composite vascular-neurodegenerative risk signature. Useful combinations may include AGT protein levels, AGT genotypes, blood pressure history, stroke markers, kidney function, diabetes/metabolic markers, inflammatory markers, APOE status, BBB biomarkers, and neuroimaging evidence of small-vessel disease.
  9. Account for sex, ancestry, tissue compartment, disease stage, and comorbidity. AGT associations vary across populations and may differ substantially between circulating, hepatic, renal, vascular, and brain-local RAS activity profiles.
  10. Exclude non-angiotensinogen "AGT" meanings from evidence synthesis. Only AGT as angiotensinogen/RAS biology is relevant to the Alzheimer's therapeutic-target hypothesis in this corpus; all other abbreviations must be filtered before drawing conclusions.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — AGT / Angiotensinogen in Alzheimer's Disease

AGT/angiotensinogen occupies a plausible but unvalidated position as an upstream modifier of Alzheimer's risk and progression. Its most compelling Alzheimer's-relevant biology flows through neurovascular and astrocyte-mediated mechanisms: senescent astrocyte AGT upregulation disrupts BBB integrity, salt-sensitive hypertension drives an AGT-linked chain culminating in phospho-tau accumulation, acetylcholine depletion, dendritic spine loss, and cognitive impairment, and proteomic network data from an ICV-STZ model places AGT within mitochondrial, synaptic, and neuroinflammatory disruption. These signals are genuinely Alzheimer's-adjacent, particularly for the large subset of patients with mixed Alzheimer's/vascular dementia phenotypes.

Nevertheless, direct evidence linking AGT to amyloid-β, tau pathology, or clinical Alzheimer's outcomes is absent from this corpus. Therapeutic tractability is strong in cardiovascular medicine — Zilebesiran's >90% AGT suppression is remarkable — but CNS efficacy and safety in older, cognitively impaired populations remain entirely untested. The most defensible near-term strategy is RAS rebalancing toward the ACE2/Ang-(1–7)/Mas axis in high-vascular-risk Alzheimer's contexts, combined with rigorous Alzheimer's-specific mechanistic and biomarker studies before any therapeutic claim is advanced.
