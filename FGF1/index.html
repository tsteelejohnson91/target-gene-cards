Overview

Safety Flag: FGF1/FGFR signaling is repeatedly associated with proliferation, angiogenesis, fibrosis, tumor growth, cancer-associated fibroblast activation, drug resistance, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and inflammatory NET formation in non-AD contexts. Uncontrolled systemic or chronic FGF1 activation could be unsafe, particularly in older patients.

FGF1 is a multifunctional fibroblast growth factor with direct but limited Alzheimer's disease evidence and extensive indirect mechanistic relevance. The strongest AD-specific findings are preclinical: FGF1/FGFR1 agonism improved spatial memory, enhanced neurogenesis, suppressed reactive astrogliosis, restricted dystrophic neurites, improved FGFR1-dependent microglial phagocytosis, and uniquely reduced senile plaque deposition compared with the FGFR1 agonist FGL in an AD mouse model.

An additional line of evidence linked early 40 Hz/5 Hz compound pulsed magnetic field (cPMF) treatment in female 5xFAD mice to improved cognition, reduced Aβ plaques and astrogliosis, and increased brain Fgf1 expression, while later treatment reduced plaques and astrogliosis without restoring cognition or Fgf1 expression — suggesting FGF1 may be tied to time-sensitive neuroprotective and plasticity responses. A streptozotocin-induced AD model implicated a proposed FGF1/PI3K/Akt pathway in neuroprotection, and a human AD network-genetics study identified FGF1 among loci influencing gray matter structural covariance networks associated with cognition.

Across the broader literature, FGF1 intersects with PI3K/AKT/mTOR, MAPK/ERK, AMPK/Nrf2, NF-κB, IL-6/STAT3, heparan sulfate biology, oxidative stress, apoptosis, senescence, autophagy/lipophagy, microglial and astrocytic function, neurogenesis, glutamate handling, angiogenesis, vascular repair, insulin/glucose regulation, lipid handling, and mitochondrial function — supporting FGF1 as a plausible but context-dependent therapeutic candidate rather than an established AD therapy.

Key Insights

Direct AD Neuroprotective Evidence

In an AD mouse model, FGF1 and FGL reversed spatial memory impairment, enhanced neurogenesis, suppressed reactive astrogliosis, and restricted dystrophic neurites. Critically, FGF1 uniquely reduced senile plaque deposition — an effect not attributable to generic FGFR1 agonism alone.

Microglial Phagocytosis Mechanism

FGF1 enhanced microglial phagocytosis in culture via an FGFR1-dependent mechanism, blocked by an FGFR1-specific inhibitor. This provides a plausible cellular route by which FGF1 could improve amyloid plaque or debris clearance in AD-like disease.

Time-Sensitivity of Benefit

In 5xFAD mice, early cPMF treatment improved cognition and upregulated Fgf1, whereas later treatment reduced plaques and astrogliosis without restoring cognition or Fgf1 expression. FGF1-linked trophic and plasticity mechanisms appear most operative before advanced network failure.

PI3K/Akt as Core Signaling Axis

A proposed FGF1/PI3K/Akt pathway in the streptozotocin AD model was associated with reduced oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, cholinergic dysfunction, β-amyloid abnormalities, and memory deficits. Broader studies additionally link FGF1 to insulin signaling, mTOR regulation, and metabolic survival pathways.

Metabolic & Vascular Relevance

Central FGF1 can durably normalize hyperglycemia in rodent diabetes models and alter hypothalamic circuits. Peripheral FGF1 improves glucose/lipid metabolism and insulin resistance via AMPK-related pathways. FGF1 is also linked to angiogenesis, endothelial repair, and cerebral perfusion — all relevant to AD risk and progression.

Substantial Safety Liabilities

FGF1/FGFR signaling is repeatedly linked in non-AD contexts to proliferation, tumor growth, cancer-associated fibroblast activation, drug resistance, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, fibrosis, stromal remodeling, and inflammatory NET formation. These liabilities require careful management in any AD therapeutic program.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Treat FGF1 as a promising but unvalidated AD target. The evidence supports mechanistic plausibility and some preclinical AD signals, but not clinical validation or definitive disease-modifying efficacy. Proceed with explicit acknowledgment of this gap.
  2. Prioritize AD-specific validation studies. Key endpoints should include cognition, amyloid-β burden and clearance, tau phosphorylation/aggregation, synaptic integrity, neurogenesis, microglial phagocytosis, astrocyte reactivity, neuroinflammation, neuronal survival, BBB integrity, cerebral perfusion, and brain metabolic function.
  3. Focus on FGFR1-dependent mechanisms. The most direct AD model evidence centers on FGFR1 agonism, FGFR1-dependent microglial phagocytosis, neurogenesis, astrogliosis suppression, dystrophic neurite restriction, and plaque reduction. Receptor subtype specificity should guide compound design.
  4. Test timing as a critical variable. Evidence from 5xFAD mice suggests FGF1-associated cognitive benefit may require early intervention. Studies should systematically compare early, mid-stage, and late-stage AD model interventions.
  5. Prefer engineered or non-mitogenic FGF1 strategies. Variants such as FGF1ΔHBS, non-mitogenic FGF1, stabilized mutants, or receptor/pathway-biased analogs may reduce proliferative and oncogenic liabilities while preserving anti-inflammatory, metabolic, vascular, and neuroprotective benefits.
  6. Use targeted CNS delivery approaches. Intranasal, intracerebroventricular, nanoparticle, exosome, hydrogel, glycosaminoglycan-mimetic, or brain-region-targeted delivery may help avoid systemic mitogenic, angiogenic, fibrotic, and cancer-related risks.
  7. Investigate FGF1 in metabolically driven AD phenotypes. Because FGF1 strongly affects diabetes, insulin resistance, lipid metabolism, and hypothalamic circuits, it may be especially relevant for AD associated with type 2 diabetes, obesity, vascular disease, or impaired brain energy metabolism.
  8. Monitor safety rigorously. Any AD-directed FGF1 program must include long-term assessment of tumor risk, abnormal angiogenesis, gliosis, fibrosis-like remodeling, edema, vascular leakage, systemic metabolic effects, and receptor desensitization.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — FGF1 in Alzheimer's Disease FGF1 is best viewed as a context-dependent neurotrophic, immunometabolic, and vascular-modulatory node with potential but as-yet unproven AD therapeutic relevance. Its most compelling AD mechanisms are FGFR1-dependent microglial phagocytosis, neurogenesis enhancement, astrogliosis suppression, amyloid plaque reduction, PI3K/Akt-mediated neuroprotection, metabolic rescue, and early-stage trophic support. The evidence base is predominantly preclinical, with supporting human network-genetics data. The principal translational challenge is preserving neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic benefits while avoiding the mitogenic, angiogenic, fibrotic, and oncogenic effects that are well-documented in FGF1/FGFR biology. Engineered variants, receptor-biased analogs, and targeted CNS delivery strategies represent the most defensible paths forward.
