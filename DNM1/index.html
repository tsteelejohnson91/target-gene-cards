DNM1 & Alzheimer's Disease

Target Gene Card — AI-Assisted Evidence Summary

Overview

⚠ Therapeutic Caution
Human DNM1 mutations cause severe neurological disease including developmental and epileptic encephalopathy. DNM1 is essential for normal synaptic function, and broad inhibition risks worsening neurotransmission and cognition. Therapeutic direction remains unresolved.

DNM1 encodes dynamin-1, a neuron-enriched GTPase central to synaptic vesicle endocytosis and recycling, membrane fission, clathrin-mediated trafficking, neurotransmitter release, and neuronal network stability. Across the available evidence, DNM1 is most consistently connected to Alzheimer's disease (AD) through synaptic dysfunction, vesicle transport, endocytic trafficking, hippocampal and cortical proteomic remodeling, cognitive impairment, and broader mitochondrial/oxidative-stress biology.

The AD-specific evidence is limited but supportive. DNM1 appears in AD-relevant proteomic or treatment-response contexts — including treadmill exercise in 3×Tg-AD mice, ellagic acid treatment in an Aβ(1–42)-induced AD model, altered protein turnover in the AppNL-F mouse model, and neurodegeneration-related proteomic signatures. In these settings DNM1 is not an established primary AD driver, but part of synaptic/endocytic and mitochondrial-stress networks overlapping with core AD mechanisms.

Many mitochondrial studies reference yeast/fungal Dnm1 or mammalian DRP1/DNM1L rather than human neuronal DNM1, so those findings support dynamin-family pathway relevance more than direct DNM1-specific AD causality. Overall, DNM1 is best viewed as a plausible AD-relevant synaptic/endocytic pathway node and potential biomarker of synaptic resilience or treatment response, not yet a validated standalone therapeutic target for Alzheimer's disease.

Mechanistic Overview

Key Insights

Core Synaptic Mechanism

DNM1's strongest AD-relevant role is synaptic vesicle recycling. It mediates membrane fission during endocytosis, sustaining neurotransmitter release cycles. Because synapse loss is an early, clinically important AD feature, altered DNM1 function could plausibly impair neurotransmission and memory.

Exercise & Ellagic Acid Response

In 3×Tg-AD treadmill exercise studies, DNM1 appeared among cortical proteins shifted toward wild-type-like profiles alongside synaptic and amyloid-pathway proteins. In an Aβ(1–42) model, ellagic acid recruited DNM1 alongside CREB and synaptophysin in the synaptic-plasticity arm, linking it to LTP and memory improvement under Aβ stress.

AppNL-F Protein Turnover

In the AppNL-F AD mouse model, disease-associated protein turnover changes converged on synaptic vesicle recycling proteins including Dnm1, Cltc, and Rims1, and mitochondrial proteins Fis1 and Ndufv1. This implicates DNM1 in AD-related synaptic proteostasis beyond simple expression change.

POAG–AD Network Overlap

DNM1 was identified among shared network genes associated with both Alzheimer's disease and primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG). This places it in a broader eye-brain neurodegeneration network involving synaptic, endocytic, and stress-response biology, though causality is not established.

Safety: DNM1 Mutation Risk

Human DNM1 mutations cause severe developmental and epileptic encephalopathy, seizures, intellectual disability, hypotonia, cortical visual impairment, and ataxia. These data confirm DNM1 function is dosage- and activity-sensitive. Broad inhibition is unlikely to be safe in AD unless a specific pathological overactivity mechanism is proven.

Mitochondrial Caveat

Many mitochondrial fission studies citing "Dnm1" describe yeast/fungal orthologs or mammalian DRP1/DNM1L, not human neuronal DNM1. These findings support dynamin-family pathway relevance to AD but do not establish DNM1 as the direct mitochondrial fission target. DRP1/DNM1L is likely the more appropriate mammalian mitochondrial target.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Treat DNM1 as a candidate node, not a validated AD-causal gene. The current evidence positions it as a plausible synaptic-resilience and endocytic-trafficking node relevant to AD, not as an established primary driver of amyloid, tau, or neuroinflammatory pathology.
  2. Prioritize AD-specific validation studies. Measure DNM1 abundance, phosphorylation, localization, isoform balance, and turnover in human AD brain, AD mouse models, and iPSC-derived neurons across disease stage and brain region.
  3. Determine therapeutic direction before any intervention. Current evidence does not establish whether DNM1 should be increased, decreased, or normalized in AD. Studies must distinguish whether DNM1 changes are compensatory, pathogenic, or secondary to synaptic degeneration.
  4. Focus on pathway normalization rather than blunt activation or inhibition. The most defensible concept is restoring appropriate DNM1-dependent synaptic vesicle recycling and endocytic trafficking while avoiding disruption of essential presynaptic function.
  5. Use synaptic endpoints in AD models. Relevant readouts include vesicle recycling kinetics, releasable vesicle pool size, synaptophysin/SYT/SNAP25/SYP markers, LTP, neurotransmitter release, excitation/inhibition balance, network hyperexcitability, and memory behavior.
  6. Separate DNM1 from DRP1/DNM1L in mitochondrial studies. If reducing mitochondrial fragmentation is the therapeutic goal in AD, DRP1/DNM1L is the more direct mammalian target; DNM1 should be evaluated primarily for neuronal endocytosis and synaptic effects unless AD-specific mitochondrial roles are demonstrated.
  7. Consider DNM1 as part of a broader presynaptic module. Relevant interacting or co-altered proteins include CLTC, RIMS1, NSF, DLG2, SYT1, SYP, SYN1/2, SNAP25, VAMP2, APP, APOE, MAP2, MAPT, DYNC1H1, FIS1, NDUFV1, OPA1, and synaptic/endocytic regulators.
  8. Explore DNM1 as a biomarker of synaptic treatment response. Exercise, ellagic acid, aging, and cognitive-impairment studies suggest DNM1 may track synaptic remodeling, plasticity, or cognitive vulnerability — making it a candidate pharmacodynamic marker.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — DNM1 in Alzheimer's Disease

DNM1 is a plausible, mechanistically motivated, but not yet validated node in Alzheimer's disease biology. Its most compelling relevance lies in presynaptic vesicle recycling and clathrin-mediated endocytosis — processes directly implicated in the earliest synaptic failure of AD. Proteomic evidence from 3×Tg-AD exercise studies, AppNL-F protein turnover analyses, and Aβ-model treatment-response data position DNM1 as a marker of synaptic remodeling and treatment response rather than a primary disease driver. The intersection with POAG–AD networks and hippocampal cognitive impairment models adds contextual support. However, the mitochondrial-fission literature must be interpreted cautiously — most findings describe DRP1/DNM1L orthologs, not human neuronal DNM1 — and genetic DNM1 disruption causes severe neurological disease, imposing meaningful therapeutic safety constraints. The most defensible current hypothesis is that DNM1-associated synaptic vesicle recycling and endocytic pathways contribute to AD synaptic failure and may be modifiable by future precision interventions, but direct evidence linking DNM1 to amyloid production, tau pathology, neuroinflammation, or AD progression remains insufficient to justify it as a primary therapeutic target without further disease-specific validation.
