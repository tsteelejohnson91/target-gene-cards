ACOT11 (also known as THEM1, BFIT2, StarD14, or brown-fat-inducible thioesterase) is not a validated Alzheimer's disease therapeutic target, but it is an emerging AD-relevant candidate because of its roles in long-chain fatty acyl-CoA hydrolysis, lipid sensing, mitochondrial lipid handling, energy metabolism, ER stress, and inflammatory cytokine regulation.

The most direct AD-related evidence is genetic: a 2023 machine-learning GWAS study using ADNI and UK Biobank data identified ACOT11 in an epistatic interaction involving APOE and SASH1. ACOT11 was not reported as a standalone genome-wide significant AD locus, but its interaction with APOE is notable because APOE is the strongest common genetic risk factor for late-onset AD and is deeply involved in lipid transport, lipid remodeling, neuroinflammation, and metabolic stress.

Mechanistically, ACOT11 hydrolyzes long-chain fatty acyl-CoA thioesters into free fatty acids and CoA. It contains two hotdog-fold thioesterase domains and a C-terminal START lipid-binding/sensing domain. Human ACOT11 has been reported to localize to the mitochondrial matrix and to associate functionally with lipid droplets and lipid ligands such as long-chain fatty acids and lysophosphatidylcholine. These properties align with biological processes central to AD pathogenesis, including mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid dyshomeostasis, lipid droplet biology, membrane remodeling, oxidative stress, ER stress, and neuroinflammation.

However, the supplied literature does not show that ACOT11 expression or activity is altered in AD brain, nor that ACOT11 directly affects amyloid-β, tau pathology, synaptic dysfunction, neuronal death, cognition, or microglial phenotypes in AD models.

Key Insights

Genetic Signal — Epistatic, Not Standalone

ACOT11 emerged from a 2023 ML-GWAS (ADNI + UK Biobank) as part of an epistatic interaction with APOE and SASH1 — not as an independent genome-wide significant AD risk locus. The SASH1 SNP reportedly reduced the pathogenic ACOT11–APOE interaction effect, underscoring a three-way dependency.

Enzymatic Role — Lipid & Energy Homeostasis

ACOT11 hydrolyzes long-chain fatty acyl-CoA thioesters into free fatty acids and CoA, directly regulating acyl-CoA, FFA, and CoA pools. This is AD-relevant because lipid metabolism and mitochondrial bioenergetics are major features of neuronal vulnerability in Alzheimer's disease.

Metabolic Knockout Phenotype — Protective Signals

In brown adipose tissue, Them1 knockout mice show increased oxygen consumption, enhanced fatty acid oxidation, heat production, resistance to diet-induced obesity, improved glucose homeostasis, and reduced inflammation and ER stress — suggesting ACOT11 inhibition may be metabolically beneficial under overload conditions.

Inflammatory Role — Context-Dependent

In macrophages, ACOT11 overexpression inhibited IL-1β maturation and altered JAK-STAT/interferon signaling via lipid-driven palmitoylation of IFNGR2. If analogous mechanisms operate in microglia, ACOT11 could modulate AD neuroinflammatory tone — but this is entirely untested in neural or AD contexts.

START Domain — Lipid Sensing & APOE Link

The C-terminal START domain supports a lipid-sensing or lipid-transfer role, potentially relevant to AD through membrane lipid remodeling, lipid droplet biology, lysophospholipid signaling, and APOE-dependent lipid transport. These properties are mechanistically aligned with AD but remain unproven for ACOT11 specifically.

Therapeutic Direction — Genuinely Uncertain

ACOT11 deletion appears beneficial in metabolic disease models, yet ACOT11 overexpression appears anti-inflammatory in macrophage models. Inhibiting vs. enhancing ACOT11 could have opposing consequences depending on cell type, disease stage, and APOE genotype. No single therapeutic strategy can be assumed without resolving these context dependencies.

Measure ACOT11 expression, protein abundance, and enzymatic activity in neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and vascular-associated cells from AD and control tissue — stratified by brain region, disease stage, and APOE genotype.

  3. Investigate the ACOT11–APOE–SASH1 genetic interaction in larger, well-powered AD cohorts. Stratify analyses by APOE ε2/ε3/ε4 status to determine whether the epistatic signal replicates and to quantify its effect size relative to APOE alone.

Evaluate mitochondrial and lipid metabolic endpoints upon ACOT11 modulation in AD-relevant neural cells: mitochondrial respiration, ATP output, fatty acyl-CoA/FFA balance, lipid droplet accumulation, oxidative stress, ferroptosis-like injury, and ER stress / unfolded protein response activation.

  5. Test ACOT11 in microglia and macrophage models relevant to AD: measure effects on IL-1β maturation, inflammasome activity, interferon/JAK-STAT signaling, IFNGR2 palmitoylation, and lipid-loaded disease-associated microglial states.

  6. Use amyloid and tau model systems to determine whether ACOT11 manipulation alters amyloid burden, tau phosphorylation and aggregation, synaptic integrity, neuronal survival, neuroinflammation, and cognitive endpoints — none of which have been reported in the current literature.

  7. Do not assume a single therapeutic strategy. ACOT11 inhibition may improve metabolic stress in some contexts, while enhancement may suppress inflammatory cytokine maturation in myeloid cells. Brain-region, cell-type, and APOE-specific effects must be resolved before any drug development rationale is established.

  8. Frame ACOT11 most plausibly as a modulator of AD risk or progression through APOE-linked lipid biology, mitochondrial fatty-acid metabolism, ER stress, and inflammatory signaling — rather than as a primary amyloid- or tau-directed target.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — ACOT11 in Alzheimer's Disease

ACOT11 is a biologically plausible but unvalidated emerging candidate for Alzheimer's disease research. Its strongest AD-relevant evidence is a machine-learning GWAS epistatic interaction with APOE and SASH1, not a standalone risk signal. Mechanistically, ACOT11's enzymatic control of long-chain fatty acyl-CoA pools, its mitochondrial matrix localization, START-domain lipid sensing, and its roles in ER stress suppression and inflammatory cytokine regulation align with known AD pathophysiology — but these connections are indirect and entirely inferred from non-AD model systems. Critically, no data demonstrate altered ACOT11 expression or activity in AD brain, nor any direct effect on amyloid-β, tau, synaptic function, neuronal survival, or cognition. The therapeutic direction is genuinely ambiguous: inhibition looks protective in metabolic overload models, while overexpression looks anti-inflammatory in macrophages, creating a cell-type- and context-dependent tension that must be resolved before any drug strategy is warranted. The highest-priority next steps are cell-type-resolved profiling in AD brain and mechanistic testing in AD-relevant neural and microglial model systems.
