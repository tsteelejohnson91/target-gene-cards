PFKP & Alzheimer's Disease

Overview

⚠ Therapeutic Caution: Broad systemic PFKP inhibition is not a safe default strategy. Because PFKP is rate-limiting for glycolysis and may be compensatory under energy stress, nonselective inhibition could worsen AD-related cerebral glucose hypometabolism or impair astrocyte and immune function. Cell-type-specific, context-aware modulation is required.

PFKP is a central, rate-limiting glycolytic enzyme and metabolic regulatory node with emerging but still limited Alzheimer's disease relevance. Across the available evidence, most findings come from non-AD systems — including cancer, immune/inflammatory disease, hypoxia, fibrosis, cardiovascular injury, infection, and metabolic stress models — repeatedly linking PFKP to glycolytic flux, lactate production, ATP balance, mitochondrial compensation, hypoxia/HIF-1α signaling, oxidative stress, inflammatory polarization, autophagy, and post-translational control.

Direct AD-relevant evidence is heterogeneous but notable. Key findings include: genetically predicted higher PFKP expression associated with increased AD risk in a lactylation-focused Mendelian randomization study; PFKP RNA editing events associated with AD dementia, neuropathology, and longitudinal cognitive decline in a large multi-region human brain cohort; hippocampal PFKP cryptic exon inclusion as part of a TDP-43 loss-of-function signature in AD neuropathologic change and LATE/AD overlap; PFKP as a conserved downstream effector of NGFR signaling in an AD neurogenic-plasticity study; and altered rhythmic PFKP expression after TDP-43 knockdown in a circadian/metabolic study.

Overall, the evidence supports PFKP as a plausible AD-relevant metabolic and stress-response node, not as a validated Alzheimer's therapeutic target. Its relevance is strongest through impaired glucose metabolism, glial inflammatory glycolysis, lactate/lactylation biology, astrocyte–neuron metabolic coupling, mitochondrial stress adaptation, autophagy/LC3-associated clearance, TDP-43-linked RNA dysregulation, and vascular/hypoxia pathways.

Mechanistic Overview

Key Insights

Central Glycolytic Control Point

PFKP catalyzes a rate-limiting step in glycolysis and is repeatedly associated with glucose uptake, fructose-1,6-bisphosphate production, lactate generation, ATP output, and extracellular acidification. This directly overlaps with AD's hallmark cerebral glucose hypometabolism and bioenergetic failure.

Direct AD-Linked Human Evidence

Higher genetically predicted PFKP expression was associated with increased AD risk in a lactylation-focused Mendelian randomization analysis. PFKP RNA editing was associated with AD dementia, neuropathologic measures, and cognitive decline in a large human brain cohort. Cryptic exon inclusion appeared in a hippocampal TDP-43 dysfunction signature in AD/LATE pathology.

Glycolysis–Lactate–Lactylation Axis

Increased PFKP can raise glycolytic flux and lactate availability. Lactate can serve as both fuel and signaling substrate, including for protein lactylation. Since lactylation has been linked to neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and tau-related biology, PFKP may connect metabolic reprogramming to epigenetic or inflammatory transcriptional changes in AD.

Glial Inflammatory Metabolism Risk

In macrophage and immune-cell studies, PFKP-driven glycolysis supports inflammatory polarization, cytokine release, and trained immunity. By analogy, excessive PFKP activity in activated microglia or reactive astrocytes could sustain neuroinflammation in AD. This remains unproven in AD microglia but is one of the strongest mechanistic hypotheses.

Astrocyte–Neuron Metabolic Support

Neuron-derived transthyretin increased astrocytic PFKP and PKM1/2 through cAMP/PKA signaling, boosting astrocytic ATP synthesis. Reduced PFKP in astrocyte injury was linked to impaired glycolysis and apoptosis. Preserving astrocytic PFKP may support neuronal energy metabolism in neurodegenerative settings.

TDP-43 / LATE Subtype Relevance

PFKP cryptic exon inclusion and altered expression/rhythmicity appear in TDP-43 dysfunction contexts. Because TDP-43 pathology frequently co-exists with AD and is associated with worse cognitive decline, PFKP may be especially relevant — and useful as a biomarker — in AD cases with LATE/TDP-43 co-pathology.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Hypothesis-generating target only. Treat PFKP as a plausible but unvalidated candidate. No supplied study proves that PFKP modulation improves amyloid, tau, synaptic function, neuroinflammation, or cognition in AD models.

  2. Prioritize cell-type-specific validation. Measure PFKP expression, activity, RNA editing, cryptic exon inclusion, and post-translational modifications across AD neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, endothelial cells, and infiltrating immune cells. Directionality likely differs by cell type.

  3. Test harmful vs. protective contexts. In activated microglia, PFKP inhibition may reduce inflammatory glycolysis. In neurons or astrocytes under mitochondrial stress, preserving or enhancing PFKP may support ATP production, lactate-mediated neuronal support, and cell survival.

  4. Investigate PFKP in TDP-43/LATE subtypes. PFKP cryptic exon inclusion and TDP-43-linked transcriptional/rhythmic changes suggest it may be particularly useful as a biomarker or mechanistic readout in AD cases with TDP-43 co-pathology.

  5. Examine the PFKP–lactate–lactylation axis. Key experiments should test whether altered PFKP increases lactate production, protein lactylation, inflammatory transcriptional programs, tau-related pathways, or mitochondrial dysfunction in AD-relevant neurons and glia.

  6. Avoid broad systemic PFKP inhibition. Because PFKP is essential for glycolysis and may be compensatory under energy stress, nonselective inhibition could worsen AD-related brain hypometabolism or impair immune/astrocyte function. Context-specific approaches are required.

  7. Consider precision upstream modulation. Rather than direct enzymatic blockade, target upstream regulators (HIF-1α, STAT1/JAK2, NFAT5, CREB/cAMP/PKA, AMPK, PI3K/AKT, mTOR, Wnt/β-catenin) or post-translational states (acetylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, lactylation, O-GlcNAcylation), or correct disease-associated RNA editing/splicing changes.

  8. Use AD-relevant models before prioritization. Required validation should include human AD brain datasets, single-cell/spatial transcriptomics and proteomics, iPSC-derived neurons/astrocytes/microglia, amyloid and tau models, TDP-43/LATE co-pathology models, and cell-specific PFKP gain- or loss-of-function studies.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — PFKP in Alzheimer's Disease

PFKP is a biologically plausible but not yet validated AD target. The weight of evidence positions it as a metabolic and stress-response node whose relevance to Alzheimer's disease is supported primarily by indirect mechanistic parallels and a small but notable set of direct human brain associations — including genetically predicted AD risk, RNA editing linked to cognitive decline, and TDP-43-associated cryptic exon inclusion. Its strongest mechanistic hypotheses involve glial inflammatory glycolysis, the lactate–lactylation–neuroinflammation axis, astrocyte–neuron metabolic coupling, and compensatory bioenergetic responses to mitochondrial dysfunction. The critical caveat is that PFKP may be protective or harmful depending on cell type and disease stage — blanket inhibition is not appropriate. The most defensible therapeutic concept is selective normalization of maladaptive PFKP-linked metabolism in specific glial or neuronal contexts, supported by rigorous cell-type-specific and AD-model validation before any therapeutic prioritization is considered.
