TMEM258 is not established as a direct Alzheimer's disease gene and has not been experimentally validated in Alzheimer's models. Its relevance to Alzheimer's disease is indirect and mechanistic, rooted in its connections to immune regulation, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, N-linked glycosylation, lipid and PUFA biology, inflammatory disease susceptibility, and the cognition–immune genetic architecture.

The strongest Alzheimer's-relevant signal derives from a 2026 cognition–immune genetics study that identified TMEM258 as one of fourteen pleiotropic genes prioritised as potential therapeutic targets for both cognitive function and autoimmune disorders. Cognitive decline and immune dysregulation are core features of Alzheimer's pathophysiology, though Alzheimer's disease itself was not the study endpoint.

At the molecular level, TMEM258 is a required component of the OST-A oligosaccharyltransferase complex, the core ER machinery responsible for N-linked glycosylation of secretory and membrane proteins. Loss of TMEM258 disrupts glycosylation, promotes unresolved ER stress and apoptosis, and drives inflammatory tissue injury in experimental intestinal models — mechanisms that overlap directly with Alzheimer's-relevant pathways including proteostasis failure, neuroinflammation, and altered protein processing.

Safety: Essential Glycosylation Role

Because TMEM258 participates in a fundamental cellular glycosylation process, broad inhibition carries a meaningful toxicity risk. Disruption can impair protein glycosylation, escalate ER stress, and trigger apoptosis — making systemic suppression a potentially harmful therapeutic strategy.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — TMEM258 in Alzheimer's Disease

TMEM258 represents an exploratory, mechanistically motivated candidate for Alzheimer's disease research rather than a validated therapeutic target. Its most compelling Alzheimer's relevance lies at the pathway level: N-linked glycosylation and ER protein quality control, neuroinflammatory and immune homeostasis, lipid and PUFA metabolism, and a cognition–immune genetic architecture that links it to cognitive traits and autoimmune disease simultaneously. The 2026 pleiotropic genetics study provides the strongest direct rationale, but Alzheimer's disease was not its endpoint. Mechanistic studies in intestinal models confirm that loss of TMEM258 causes glycosylation failure, ER stress, and apoptosis — findings that map onto core Alzheimer's pathophysiology but have not been tested in neural or Alzheimer's-specific contexts. A significant therapeutic safety concern exists: because TMEM258 is an integral subunit of the OST-A complex performing essential cellular glycosylation, broad inhibitory strategies carry a real toxicity risk. Any future therapeutic direction should focus on restoring or normalising TMEM258-dependent pathways rather than suppressing the protein globally, and must be preceded by dedicated Alzheimer's brain and model-system studies to determine whether TMEM258 genuinely modifies disease-relevant biology.
