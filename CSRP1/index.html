Overview

⚠ Caveat — Not a Validated AD Target

CSRP1 is not validated as an Alzheimer's disease therapeutic target. No direct evidence links it to amyloid-β, tau pathology, AD patient brain tissue, AD animal models, or AD-specific treatment studies. All AD relevance is indirect and hypothesis-generating.

The strongest Alzheimer's-relevant rationale connects CSRP1 to vascular aging, blood-pressure regulation, endothelial morphology, blood–brain barrier–adjacent mechanisms, glial–vascular interactions, myelination, cytoskeletal remodeling, RNA regulation, immune modulation, and metabolic/epigenetic pathways. These processes overlap substantially with major AD mechanisms — especially neurovascular dysfunction, chronic neuroinflammation, white-matter injury, and age-related cellular stress.

CSRP1 appears most plausible as a candidate modifier of the vascular–glial–myelin axis rather than a core amyloid or tau effector. Its therapeutic potential remains uncertain because its effects are highly context-dependent: CSRP1 repression may be beneficial in some vascular aging or injury settings, while CSRP1 expression may support neuronal structural plasticity or repair-like programs in other contexts.

Key Insights

Direct AD Evidence Is Absent

No study establishes CSRP1 as an AD gene, biomarker, amyloid/tau regulator, or disease-modifying target. It does not appear in AD patient brain tissue, AD animal models, or synaptic-loss assays in AD contexts. All relevance is indirect and hypothesis-generating.

Vascular Aging — Strongest Indirect Link

CSRP1-containing CpG loci were associated with age-dependent systolic and pulse-pressure changes in large epigenome-wide association studies. Focal adhesion, actin cytoskeleton remodeling, and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways were enriched, intersecting with cerebrovascular aging mechanisms relevant to AD risk.

Blood–Brain Barrier Cell Architecture

In vascular endothelial studies, CSRP1 regulated MYO10 alternative splicing, endothelial cell shape, intercellular junction widening, and vascular permeability. If analogous mechanisms operate in brain endothelium, CSRP1 could affect BBB integrity and neuroinflammation — though this is untested in AD.

Glial & Myelin Biology — CNS Connection

In mouse brain development, astrocytic PD-L1/PD-1 signaling regulated CSRP1 via MEK/ERK, affecting astrocyte morphology, end-foot coverage of blood vessels, OPC migration, myelination, and cognition — mechanisms relevant to white-matter damage and neurovascular coupling in AD.

Neuronal Structural Plasticity

Increased CRP1/CSRP1 protein was associated with greater hippocampal dendritic length, dendritic complexity, and CA1 spine density in a melatonin study in mice and cultured hippocampal neurons. This is relevant to AD synaptic degeneration but was not conducted in an AD model.

Unresolved Directionality — Context Dependency

CSRP1 is downregulated in some neurodegeneration/vascular signatures, repressed during FOXO3-mediated vascular protection, upregulated in some cancers, and associated with neuronal dendritic complexity in other contexts. A systemic activator or inhibitor could therefore produce mixed or harmful effects, and cell-type specificity must be resolved before targeting.

RNA Regulation & Epitranscriptomic Roles

CSRP1 was validated as a tissue-dependent RNA-binding protein capable of modulating MYO10 alternative splicing. m6A-mediated mRNA stabilization of CSRP1 promoted glycolysis. Given widespread RNA-binding protein dysfunction and altered splicing in AD, this is a plausible but untested mechanism.

Metabolic & Epigenetic Intersections

CSRP1 appears in lactylation-related signatures in atherosclerosis and m6A-glycolysis pathways in acute myeloid leukemia. Since AD involves impaired glucose metabolism, glial metabolic reprogramming, and epigenetic dysregulation, CSRP1 may be a node connecting these axes — though extrapolated from non-AD systems.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — Mechanistically Plausible but Unproven AD-Adjacent Candidate

CSRP1 is best viewed as a mechanistically plausible but unproven AD-adjacent target candidate. Its strongest potential lies in vascular aging, blood–brain barrier regulation, astrocyte–vascular interactions, myelination, cytoskeletal remodeling, RNA regulation, and immune-metabolic signaling, rather than in direct amyloid or tau modulation. The most immediate research priority is establishing whether CSRP1 is dysregulated in human AD brain or vasculature and whether its manipulation in AD-relevant cell types produces phenotypes consistent with disease mechanisms. Directionality is a critical unresolved issue: context-dependent up- and downregulation across cell types, diseases, and experimental conditions means that any therapeutic strategy must be preceded by rigorous, cell-type-specific functional characterization. Until such data exist, CSRP1 is more valuable as a candidate pathway node and biomarker anchor than as a direct drug target.
