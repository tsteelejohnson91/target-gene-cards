Key Insights

Acronym Ambiguity — Critical Distinction

"RET" in the evidence base refers to at least three unrelated entities: the RET receptor tyrosine kinase, mitochondrial reverse electron transport, and resonance energy transfer used in biosensors. Merging these in any AD interpretation would be a fundamental analytical error.

Mito-RET — Most AD-Relevant Mechanism

Mitochondrial reverse electron transport has the clearest connection to AD pathology in the text. Tau enters mitochondria, binds NDUFS3 (complex I), drives reverse electron flow, elevates ROS, and lowers NAD⁺/NADH — reinforcing tau hyperphosphorylation in a self-amplifying neurodegenerative loop.

Potential Mito-RET Intervention Strategies

Cross-model evidence supports hypothesis-generating therapeutic directions: reducing pathological complex I reverse electron flow, blocking tau mitochondrial entry, disrupting tau–NDUFS3 binding, or restoring the mitochondrial NAD⁺/NADH balance. These are exploratory, not validated targets.

RET RTK — Not an Established AD Target

The RET receptor tyrosine kinase is a well-validated precision oncology target (thyroid cancer, medullary thyroid carcinoma, NSCLC) but the text provides no evidence it modifies amyloid-β, tau pathology, synaptic loss, cognition, or any clinical AD endpoint.

RET RTK — Indirect Neurobiological Plausibility Only

RET/GDNF/GFRα signaling through PI3K/AKT, MAPK/ERK, CREB, and STAT3 overlaps conceptually with neuronal survival and stress biology. If studied in AD, enhancing rather than inhibiting RET signaling might be more plausible, but this is entirely speculative without AD-specific evidence.

ECL-RET — Diagnostic Technology, Not a Drug Target

Electrochemiluminescence resonance energy transfer (ECL-RET) platforms represent a diagnostic signal-transfer technology for early detection and monitoring of AD biomarkers — specifically p-Tau181, amyloid-β, and hyperphosphorylated tau. This is relevant to diagnostics, not therapeutics.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Do not treat RET RTK as an established AD target. The supplied evidence does not support claims that RET mutations, fusions, expression changes, or RET inhibitors modify any aspect of Alzheimer's disease biology or clinical progression.
  2. Separate the three "RET" acronyms rigorously. RET receptor tyrosine kinase, mitochondrial reverse electron transport, and resonance energy transfer are biologically unrelated and must not be conflated in any AD evidence review or target assessment.
  3. Prioritize mitochondrial reverse electron transport for AD mechanistic follow-up. It has the clearest textual link to tau pathology, oxidative stress, NAD⁺/NADH imbalance, aging biology, and neurodegeneration-like phenotypes in model systems.
  4. Treat mito-RET interventions as hypothesis-generating, not validated. Candidate strategies — selective complex I RET reduction, prevention of tau mitochondrial entry, tau–NDUFS3 disruption, ROS reduction, NAD⁺/NADH restoration — require rigorous AD-focused experimental validation.
  5. Consider RET RTK only as an exploratory neurotrophic-signaling candidate. AD-focused research would need to first confirm whether RET/GDNF/GFRα signaling is altered in AD brain regions and whether it affects amyloid, tau, synaptic, inflammatory, or cognitive endpoints in AD models.
  6. Avoid direct repurposing assumptions from oncology. Selpercatinib and pralsetinib demonstrate pharmacological tractability and CNS penetrance, but not AD suitability. Chronic RET inhibition in older adults may carry safety risks (hypertension, hepatotoxicity, QTc prolongation, ILD) and could interfere with beneficial neurotrophic signaling.
  7. Leverage ECL-RET platforms for AD diagnostics, not therapeutics. Resonance energy transfer biosensors offer a technology lead for early detection and monitoring of p-Tau181, amyloid-β, and hyperphosphorylated tau — valuable for biomarker strategy but irrelevant to drug target selection.
