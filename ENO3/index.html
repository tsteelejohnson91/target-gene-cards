ENO3 & Alzheimer's Disease

Target Enabling Package — AI-Assisted Evidence Summary

Overview

⚠ Safety Concern — Skeletal Muscle Toxicity

ENO3 loss-of-function mutations cause Glycogen Storage Disease Type XIII (GSD-XIII), characterised by exercise intolerance, recurrent rhabdomyolysis, impaired lactate production, glycogen accumulation, and myalgias. Chronic systemic ENO3 inhibition is therefore likely to carry significant muscle toxicity risk.

ENO3 (β-enolase / Enolase 3) is classically described as a muscle-enriched glycolytic enzyme catalysing the conversion of 2-phosphoglycerate to phosphoenolpyruvate, but the accumulated literature situates it in a far broader landscape of stress-response biology — spanning hypoxia/HIF signalling, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, ferroptosis and iron handling, lipid metabolism, vascular remodelling, and insulin resistance.

Direct Alzheimer's disease (AD) evidence is suggestive but not definitive. ENO3 was identified among six shared glucometabolic genes across AD and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) datasets; it was upregulated in an AD mouse model; and it emerged as a hub gene in an APOE ε4-carrier-specific AD co-expression module. These findings position ENO3 as a candidate metabolic biomarker or network node, not a validated causal AD gene or established therapeutic target.

ENO3 should not be interpreted as simply beneficial or harmful. Upregulation can support pathological glycolysis, proliferation, vascular remodelling, inflammation, and ferroptotic disease progression in some settings, while restoration or enhancement is associated with improved barrier function, insulin signalling, or reduced ferroptotic injury in others. Any therapeutic direction in AD would be highly cell-type-, disease-stage-, and context-dependent.

Mechanistic Overview — ENO3 in AD-Relevant Pathways

Key Insights

AD Evidence — Suggestive, Not Definitive

ENO3 was one of six shared glucometabolic genes across AD and T2DM datasets (alongside G6PD, PKM, PFKL, PGD, TALDO1), was upregulated in an AD murine model, and appeared as a hub gene in an APOE ε4-specific co-expression module with ISOC1, GDF10, GNB3, XPO4, ACLY, and MATN2. These are network associations, not causal proofs.

Glycolytic Remodelling — Strongest Mechanistic Link

ENO3 catalyses the conversion of 2-phosphoglycerate to phosphoenolpyruvate and consistently tracks glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway signatures. Given that AD involves impaired cerebral glucose utilisation and altered bioenergetics, ENO3 may reflect or influence metabolic compensation or maladaptation in vulnerable brain and vascular cells.

APOE ε4 — Context-Specific Network Hub

ENO3-containing hub-gene clusters became correlated in a time-dependent manner under APOE ε4 treatment but not APOE ε3 treatment. This suggests ENO3 participates in APOE ε4-linked transcriptional and metabolic remodelling rather than representing a universal AD marker, making APOE genotype stratification essential in any future study.

Hypoxia / Neurovascular Stress

ENO3 repeatedly appears in hypoxia-related signatures and HIF-1-linked programmes across pulmonary hypertension, diabetic retinopathy, preeclampsia, cancer, and high-altitude adaptation. In AD, cerebral hypoperfusion, sleep-disordered breathing, and vascular dysfunction converge on similar stress pathways — an untested but biologically plausible ENO3 connection.

Ferroptosis / Iron–Lipid Peroxidation Bridge

ENO3 has been linked to ferroptosis-related signatures involving PKM2, GPX4, SLC7A11, Fe²⁺, MDA, and IRP1 in non-brain models. Because AD implicates iron dyshomeostasis, glutathione/GPX4 impairment, lipid peroxidation, and ferroptosis-like neuronal injury, the ENO3–PKM2, ENO3–GPX4, and ENO3–IRP1 axes merit direct investigation in AD-relevant systems.

Safety — Myopathy is a Hard Constraint

ENO3 is essential for skeletal muscle glycolysis; loss-of-function mutations cause GSD Type XIII with exercise intolerance, recurrent rhabdomyolysis, and glycogen accumulation. Systemic ENO3 inhibition is likely to create severe muscle toxicity, making brain- or cell-type-targeted modulation or indirect pathway approaches mandatory for any therapeutic strategy.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Treat ENO3 as hypothesis-generating, not validated. Current evidence supports metabolic plausibility and some AD/T2DM or APOE ε4 network associations but not causal involvement in amyloid-β, tau, synaptic failure, neurodegeneration, cognition, or clinical AD progression.
  2. Prioritise AD-specific expression validation. Measure ENO3 expression, protein abundance, post-translational modifications, and localisation in AD brain regions, stratified by APOE genotype, Braak stage, amyloid/tau burden, sex, metabolic comorbidity, and vascular pathology.
  3. Resolve cell-type specificity before any therapeutic claim. Test ENO3 across neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, endothelial cells, pericytes, and cerebrovascular smooth muscle cells — neuronal ENO3 may support energy metabolism while microglial or vascular ENO3 may drive inflammatory glycolysis or remodelling.
  4. Test AD-relevant stressors experimentally. Determine whether amyloid-β, phosphorylated tau, APOE ε4, hypoxia, inflammatory cytokines, insulin resistance, iron overload, lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial stress, or sleep/stress-hormone conditions alter ENO3 expression or activity.
  5. Investigate the six high-priority regulatory axes. APOE ε4–ENO3 network regulation, miR-34a/ENO3 insulin-metabolic signalling, SIRT6–ENO3 vascular glycolytic control, ENO3–PKM2 ferroptosis/lipid metabolism, ENO3–IRP1 iron handling, and ENO3–GPX4 ferroptosis resistance are the most mechanistically grounded entry points.
  6. Consider ENO3 as a biomarker before a drug target. Based on current evidence, ENO3 may be more immediately useful as a marker of metabolic stress, APOE ε4-associated network activity, peripheral metabolic dysfunction, or AD/T2DM overlap than as a directly targeted enzyme.
  7. Never assume universal inhibition or activation. Any therapeutic approach must define whether the goal is to restore neuronal bioenergetics, suppress inflammatory glycolysis, reduce vascular remodelling, or modulate ferroptosis — the same ENO3 intervention could be beneficial in one cellular compartment and harmful in another.
  8. Account for peripheral muscle safety as a hard constraint. Because ENO3 deficiency causes GSD-XIII myopathy, systemic inhibition is likely risky. Brain- or cell-type-targeted modulation, indirect pathway modulation via regulatory axes, or biomarker use are substantially safer approaches than broad ENO3 blockade.

Overall Assessment

ENO3 is a biologically plausible, metabolism-centred AD candidate with limited direct mechanistic support. Its strongest AD-related rationale lies in cerebral glucose hypometabolism, APOE ε4-associated metabolic network remodelling, hypoxia and neurovascular stress, inflammatory immunometabolism in glial cells, and the ferroptosis/iron–lipid peroxidation pathway. The shared glucometabolic gene signature across AD and T2DM datasets, the upregulation in an AD mouse model, and the APOE ε4-specific co-expression hub status each add modest, converging plausibility — but none constitutes direct causal evidence. Therapeutic ambidirectionality (ENO3 may need to be enhanced in some compartments and suppressed in others) and the muscle toxicity safety liability of systemic manipulation are the primary translational barriers. Direct AD validation across human tissue, relevant animal models, and cell-type-resolved systems is required before ENO3 can be considered a genuine therapeutic target; in the near term, its most defensible utility is as a metabolic and network biomarker in APOE ε4-stratified or AD/T2DM comorbid populations.
