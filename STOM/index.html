STOM / Stomatin & Alzheimer's Disease

Target Enabling Package — AI-Assisted Evidence Summary

Overview

Caution — Limited Direct AD Evidence: STOM is not established as a validated Alzheimer's disease therapeutic target. Direct AD evidence is sparse and inconsistent; the mechanistic links below are largely inferred from non-AD systems. Systemic modulation carries pleiotropic risk given broad tissue expression.

STOM (stomatin) is a membrane-associated, lipid raft/microdomain scaffold protein belonging to the SPFH-domain superfamily. It forms oligomeric ring/cage-like assemblies that stiffen and organize local membrane domains, regulate transporter and channel complexes, and couple membranes to the cytoskeleton. These properties intersect conceptually with several Alzheimer's disease mechanisms, including cholesterol-rich lipid raft organization, APP-processing compartment biology, endosomal/lysosomal trafficking, synaptic membrane architecture, ion-channel homeostasis, glucose hypometabolism, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and neurovascular dysfunction.

The most defensible interpretation of the available evidence is that STOM is an AD-associated biomarker candidate and a mechanistically plausible modifier of AD-relevant pathways — not a proven AD driver or validated therapeutic target. No supplied study demonstrates that STOM modulation alters amyloid-β, tau, synaptic degeneration, cognition, or AD progression in a disease-relevant model.

Key Insights

AD Proteomics Signal

STOM appeared in a large AD brain proteomics/machine-learning classifier (6 cohorts, 620 subjects, 3,334 proteins) predicting AD versus control status, alongside APP, NRXN1, RABEP1, and VGF. The classifier was enriched for sugar metabolism, suggesting STOM may reflect metabolic remodelling in clinically apparent AD rather than a causal mechanism.

Tauopathy-Adjacent Evidence

A progressive supranuclear palsy systems-biology study prioritised glial DDR2, STOM, and KANK2 as therapeutic targets. PSP is a tauopathy with glial involvement, lending indirect plausibility to STOM's relevance in AD tau pathology and glial dysfunction hypotheses — though no direct AD-tau evidence is provided.

STOM–GLUT1 Axis & Glucose Hypometabolism

STOM can bind GLUT1 and reduce glucose transport activity without reducing membrane GLUT1 abundance. Because cerebral glucose hypometabolism is a core AD phenotype and GLUT1 is critical at the blood-brain barrier, STOM could theoretically contribute to AD-related energy deficits in endothelial, astrocytic, or neuronal contexts — an untested but mechanistically strong hypothesis.

Channel & Transporter Regulation

STOM regulates ASIC gating and inhibits pannexin-1 currents in primary mouse astrocytes without altering surface Panx1 abundance. It also interacts with aquaporin-1, AE1/SLC4A1, NTCP, and others — intersecting with AD-related ionic dyshomeostasis, astrocyte ATP signalling, pH stress, and excitotoxicity mechanisms.

Oxidative Stress & Mitochondrial Links

In non-AD models, STOM appears among mitochondria-related inflammatory gene signatures and can promote ROS accumulation, including via Prdx1 degradation in osteoclasts. Since oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction are central to AD pathology, this constitutes a plausible but entirely unvalidated STOM–ROS hypothesis.

Major Limitations & Therapeutic Risk

No evidence demonstrates STOM manipulation alters Aβ production, APP processing, tau phosphorylation, synaptic toxicity, neuronal survival, microglial phagocytosis, or cognitive decline. STOM's broad expression in erythrocytes, platelets, immune cells, and epithelia means systemic modulation could produce substantial pleiotropic off-target effects.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Treat STOM as a hypothesis-generating candidate. Do not advance it as a validated therapeutic target. Current evidence supports mechanistic exploration, not clinical translation.
  2. Establish cell-type-specific AD expression profiles. Measure STOM expression and localisation in AD versus control brain across neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, endothelial cells, and pericytes in hippocampus, temporal cortex, frontal cortex, and vascular compartments.
  3. Map colocalisation with AD membrane machinery. Determine whether STOM colocalises with APP, BACE1, γ-secretase/presenilin complexes, cholesterol-rich rafts, GM1 domains, endosomes, lysosomes, Rab11/Rab7 vesicles, and extracellular vesicle compartments.
  4. Test STOM modulation on core AD phenotypes. Assess effects on Aβ generation, aggregation, secretion, uptake, and clearance; tau phosphorylation, aggregation, and spreading; synaptic function; neuronal survival; glial cytokine release; lysosomal/autophagic flux; and cognition in validated AD models.
  5. Investigate the STOM–GLUT1 mechanism in AD-relevant cells. Focus on brain endothelial cells and astrocytes to determine whether STOM contributes to cerebral glucose hypometabolism or blood-brain barrier energy deficits under AD-like conditions.
  6. Evaluate STOM in glial biology. Examine microglial phagocytosis, phagosome maturation, lysosomal handling, inflammatory activation, and astrocytic pannexin-1/ATP signalling — each plausible STOM-regulated process relevant to AD neuroinflammation.
  7. Explore the oxidative-stress axis in neural cells. Test whether STOM regulates Prdx1, ROS accumulation, mitochondrial function, or cellular vulnerability to Aβ/tau-associated oxidative injury in neurons and glia.
  8. Assess neurovascular relevance. Determine whether STOM alters brain endothelial barrier integrity, VEGFA/ERK signalling, inflammatory adhesion, angiogenic responses, and vascular permeability under AD-like metabolic or inflammatory stress.
  9. Consider therapeutic risk from the outset. STOM's broad expression profile in erythrocytes, platelets, immune cells, and epithelia demands that any therapeutic strategy employ cell-specific or pathway-specific targeting rather than systemic modulation.
  10. Use current evidence only to justify mechanistic studies. The critical next step is determining whether STOM is causal, compensatory, or merely a biomarker within AD-relevant membrane, metabolic, inflammatory, and tau/glial pathways.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment

STOM / stomatin is a biologically compelling but evidentially immature Alzheimer's disease candidate. Its core identity as a lipid raft scaffold, oligomeric membrane organizer, and transporter/channel regulator places it at the intersection of multiple AD-relevant processes — cholesterol membrane dynamics, glucose hypometabolism, ionic dyshomeostasis, neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and neurovascular dysfunction. Its appearance in an AD brain proteomics classifier and its prioritisation in a tauopathy systems-biology study provide limited but non-trivial signals. However, no direct evidence links STOM to Aβ, tau, synaptic degeneration, or cognitive decline, and the mechanistic inferences draw almost entirely from erythrocyte, osteoclast, endothelial, and immune-cell systems. Therapeutic potential exists conceptually, but broad expression across non-neural tissues introduces substantial pleiotropic risk. The justified next step is rigorous AD-specific mechanistic validation — not target advancement.
