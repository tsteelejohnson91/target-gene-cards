MRPL30 is a mitochondrial ribosomal protein implicated in Alzheimer's disease (AD) principally through indirect evidence relating to mitochondrial translation, cellular metabolism, and cognitive resilience. In the AD-specific literature, MRPL30 was identified as one of ten candidate biomarker genes capable of distinguishing asymptomatic Alzheimer's disease (AsymAD) from symptomatic AD in human brain cohorts. AsymAD describes individuals who carry full AD neuropathology — amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles — yet remain cognitively normal.

MRPL30 is not an established causal AD gene nor a validated therapeutic target. Its relevance is strongest as a network-associated marker of resilience, suggesting that preserved mitochondrial protein synthesis and metabolic function may help certain individuals tolerate AD pathology without cognitive decline.

Non-AD studies associate MRPL30 with translation-related protein interactions and type 2 diabetes-associated differential expression. These connections remain indirect for AD but support a broader role in systemic metabolic disease contexts.

Not a Causal or Validated Target

MRPL30 is not an established causal AD gene. Its strongest role is as a network-associated marker of resilience. The mechanistic interpretation is that it may mark or contribute to preserved mitochondrial translation in cognitively resilient brains, rather than drive disease onset.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — MRPL30 in Alzheimer's Disease

MRPL30 represents a biologically plausible but indirect resilience-associated marker in Alzheimer's disease, emerging from transcriptomic profiling of asymptomatic versus symptomatic AD in well-characterised human brain cohorts. The consistent co-identification with MRPL1 lends credibility to the broader hypothesis that preserved mitoribosomal function and mitochondrial translation capacity contribute to cognitive resilience in the face of established AD neuropathology. However, causal evidence is absent, and the protein's likely essential, nonredundant nature renders it a high-risk direct therapeutic target.
