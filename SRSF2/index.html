Overview

⚠ Caution: Not an Established Therapeutic Target

SRSF2's relevance to Alzheimer's disease is mechanistic and emerging, not clinically established. Broad systemic SRSF2 inhibition poses high risk due to its core, essential roles across neuronal, glial, immune, and vascular cell types. Therapeutic concepts should focus on selective downstream splicing events or pathway modulation only after AD-specific validation.

SRSF2 is a serine/arginine-rich RNA-splicing factor whose connection to Alzheimer's disease is grounded in RNA-processing disruption in AD-relevant brain regions, tau-associated pathology, and amyloid-associated nuclear RNA dysregulation. Direct AD-related findings include SRSF2 copurification with amyloid in a proteomic study, involvement in tau exon splicing and isoform regulation, increased SRSF2 protein in posterior cingulate cortex in mild/moderate AD with association to cognitive decline, reduced nuclear SRSF2 intensity in AT8-positive phosphorylated-tau neurons across entorhinal-hippocampal regions, and a proposed GSK3β–SRSF2 mechanism influencing MAPT/Tau intron 12 retention.

The broader literature situates SRSF2 in hematologic malignancy, clonal hematopoiesis, cancer, stress biology, and RNA-processing regulation — domains whose mechanistic overlaps with AD-relevant biology (tau pathology, amyloid assemblies, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, ferroptosis, neuroinflammation, vascular aging) make SRSF2 a compelling candidate RNA-processing node. It should be treated as such rather than as a validated drug target. Its therapeutic potential is strongest as a guide to specific downstream splicing events.

Key Insights

Amyloid–RNA Processing Link

SRSF2 was identified as copurifying with amyloid in a proteomic study. If amyloid-associated assemblies sequester or functionally alter SRSF2, normal splicing programmes could be broadly disrupted — connecting amyloid pathology to nuclear RNA-processing failure.

GSK3β–SRSF2–Tau Splicing Axis

GSK3β inhibition modulates SRSF2 to promote MAPT/Tau intron 12 retention, producing a human-specific truncated Tau isoform that is less aggregation-prone. This truncated isoform is decreased in AD brains, defining a tractable and specific therapeutic hypothesis distinct from broad SRSF2 inhibition.

Cognitive Decline Without Classical Pathology

SRSF2 protein was significantly increased in posterior cingulate cortex in mild/moderate AD and associated with cognitive decline, but not with amyloid plaque or neurofibrillary tangle density — suggesting an independent RNA-processing contribution to cognitive deterioration.

Nuclear SRSF2 Loss in τ-Positive Neurons

In entorhinal-hippocampal regions, SRSF2 nuclear intensity was significantly reduced in AT8-positive (phosphorylated-tau) cells. This supports a "dual-hit" model in which cytoplasmic phospho-tau accumulation coincides with loss of nuclear splicing-factor integrity.

Vulnerability to Functional Disruption

SRSF2 function is sensitive to mutation (P95H alters RNA binding and splice-site recognition), methylglyoxal glycation, lactylation, methylation, and phosphorylation — any of which can alter stability, solubility, localization, and downstream splicing outcomes in ways relevant to disease.

Clonal Hematopoiesis — Indirect AD Route

SRSF2 mutations are recurrent in age-related CHIP with co-occurring TET2, ASXL1, DNMT3A, and other alterations. SRSF2-mutant CHIP could theoretically influence AD through systemic inflammation, monocyte/macrophage skewing, vascular injury, or blood-brain immune crosstalk — though no direct AD risk increase has been established epidemiologically.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Treat as exploratory mechanism, not validated target. SRSF2 should be regarded as a candidate RNA-processing node in AD — not a validated therapeutic target — until robust AD-specific evidence accumulates.
  2. Validate in human AD tissue across priority regions. Prioritise posterior cingulate cortex, entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, precuneus, and frontal cortex for expression, nuclear localisation, phosphorylation state, and post-translational modification profiling.
  3. Profile cell-type specificity thoroughly. Determine whether SRSF2 changes are confined to neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, endothelial cells, or infiltrating immune cells before pursuing any intervention strategy.
  4. Map AD-relevant SRSF2-dependent splicing targets. Focus especially on MAPT/Tau transcripts, synaptic genes, mitochondrial genes, proteostasis/autophagy genes, inflammatory regulators, and stress-response transcripts.
  5. Investigate amyloid-associated SRSF2 mechanistically. Determine whether copurification represents sequestration, recruitment, aggregation, or functional impairment — each has distinct therapeutic implications.
  6. Pursue the GSK3β–SRSF2–Tau intron 12 axis as a focused hypothesis. Target the specific Tau splicing outcome (intron 12 retention) rather than globally altering SRSF2 activity. Test whether restoring nuclear SRSF2 function in AT8-positive neurons normalises splicing or reduces neuronal stress.
  7. Assess SRSF2-mutant CHIP in longitudinal cohorts. Examine whether SRSF2 CHIP mutations correlate with AD incidence, vascular cognitive impairment, neuroinflammatory biomarkers, or amyloid/tau burden.
  8. Avoid broad SRSF2 inhibition unless strong safety data emerge. More realistic approaches include transcript-specific antisense modulation, correction of pathogenic splice isoforms, stabilisation of normal SRSF2 nuclear localisation, or targeting downstream inflammatory/metabolic pathways.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — SRSF2 is a biologically plausible but mechanistically early-stage candidate in Alzheimer's disease. The convergence of amyloid copurification, tau isoform regulation via the GSK3β–SRSF2 axis, increased protein in AD cortex correlating with cognitive decline, and nuclear loss in phospho-tau-positive neurons constitutes a coherent, multi-modal signal meriting serious investigation. However, none of these observations yet rises to the level of validated therapeutic target. SRSF2's broad essential roles in RNA splicing across all cell types mean that direct systemic inhibition carries substantial safety risk. The most promising near-term path is mechanistic validation in human AD tissue followed by selective targeting of the specific downstream splicing events — above all, MAPT intron 12 retention — rather than SRSF2 itself. Clonal haematopoiesis links and stress-pathway intersections represent additional hypotheses worth epidemiological and experimental investigation in the AD context.
