EPHX2 & Alzheimer's Disease

Target Gene Card — AI-Assisted Evidence Summary

Overview

Caution — Context Dependence: Complete EPHX2 loss or broad sEH inhibition is not universally beneficial. Evidence indicates possible impairment of macrophage antibacterial clearance, potential worsening of certain injury contexts via compensatory 20-HETE pathways, and protective roles for some DHA-derived diol metabolites (e.g., 19,20-DHDP) in astrocyte survival. EPHX2's N-terminal phosphatase domain may also have biology distinct from the hydrolase domain. Therapeutic programs must distinguish acute versus chronic inhibition and brain versus peripheral effects.

EPHX2 encodes soluble epoxide hydrolase (sEH), a lipid-metabolizing enzyme that hydrolyzes protective epoxy-fatty acids — especially arachidonic-acid-derived epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs), including 8,9-EET, 11,12-EET, and 14,15-EET — into less active diols such as DHETs. Across the literature, EPHX2/sEH is repeatedly positioned at the intersection of Alzheimer's disease (AD)-relevant mechanisms: neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, glial activation, cerebrovascular dysfunction, amyloid-β handling, tau pathology, synaptic injury, mitochondrial/ER stress, lipid mediator imbalance, and cognitive decline.

The strongest AD-specific evidence supports EPHX2/sEH inhibition as a disease-mechanism-facing therapeutic strategy. Multiple preclinical AD models report that sEH inhibition or Ephx2 deletion preserves EET signaling and improves outcomes involving cognition, amyloid burden, tau pathology, neuronal survival, glial function, and cerebrovascular function. Human genetic and multi-omics studies further implicate EPHX2 as a druggable or disease-relevant gene, including Mendelian randomization/proteomic evidence and AD-associated regulatory networks.

Therapeutically, the most consistent strategy is inhibition of sEH. Named inhibitors include TPPU, PTUPB, AUDA, t-AUCB, UB-EV-52, Sar5399, GSK2256294/GSK2256294A, and other experimental compounds. TPPU is the most repeatedly cited brain/AD-relevant inhibitor; PTUPB is notable as a dual sEH/COX-2 inhibitor with cognitive and cerebrovascular benefit in TgF344-AD rats.

Mechanism Diagram

Panel A (Substrate Axis): Arachidonic acid is converted to protective EETs by CYP epoxygenases; EPHX2/sEH degrades EETs to inactive DHETs. sEH inhibitors (TPPU, PTUPB, AUDA) block this step and preserve EET levels. Panel B (Neuroinflammation): Loss of EET signaling activates NF-κB/NLRP3, drives inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), microglial reactivity, and oxidative/lipid stress — each suppressed by sEH inhibition. Panel C (Amyloid/Tau): Preserved 14,15-EET enhances astrocyte lysosomal biogenesis and amyloid-β clearance, reduces tau pathology via microglial phagocytosis, and maintains synaptic density. Panel D (Cerebrovascular/Outcome): EETs regulate cerebral artery myogenic tone and neurovascular coupling (functional hyperemia); together with synaptic and anti-inflammatory inputs from Panels B/C, these converge on improved cognitive outcomes.

Key Insights

Core Mechanism — sEH–EET Axis

EPHX2/sEH degrades EETs and related epoxy-fatty acids, limiting lipid mediators that are anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, vasodilatory, cytoprotective, and pro-resolving. Inhibiting sEH shifts signaling toward neurovascular and anti-inflammatory protection, placing this axis at the heart of AD-relevant lipid biology.

Direct AD Model Evidence — Amyloid & Cognition

In 5×FAD mice, TPPU or Ephx2 deletion reduced amyloid-β deposition, improved cognition, and prevented astrocyte reactivation via increased brain 14,15-EET and enhanced astrocytic lysosomal biogenesis. In Aβ-induced AD mice, TPPU improved spatial learning, stabilized EETs, reduced neuronal death, and modulated GSK3β-linked NF-κB, p53, and Nrf2 signaling.

Tauopathy Evidence — Neurons & Microglia

In PS19 tauopathy mice, TPPU and Ephx2 knockout improved cognition, rescued neuronal loss, reduced tau pathology, and reversed disease-associated microglial states (snRNA-seq: restored actin cytoskeleton and excitatory synaptic programs). In iPSC-derived neurons, TPPU enhanced synaptic density independently of tau load. EET supplementation promoted microglial tau phagocytosis.

Cerebrovascular Therapeutic Axis

In TgF344-AD rats, oral PTUPB (dual sEH/COX-2 inhibitor) improved recognition memory, restored middle cerebral artery myogenic responses, increased arterial distensibility, and restored functional hyperemia — supporting EPHX2/sEH as a target for the vascular component of AD and mixed dementia. Elevated cortical 14,15-DHET and enhanced sEH immunoreactivity in human vascular cognitive impairment further confirm this axis.

Human Genetics & Multi-Omics Druggability

Druggable-genome Mendelian randomization identified EPHX2 as an AD-related target with evidence in blood and brain datasets and possible mediation through hippocampal volume. Brain proteome MR analyses implicated EPHX2 protein abundance as potentially causal in AD. EPHX2 also appears in AD regulatory networks including the CLU/PTK2B enhancer region and cross-trait GWAS analyses involving ADRD.

Context Dependence — Key Caveat

Complete EPHX2 loss is not uniformly beneficial. Evidence identifies possible impairment of macrophage antibacterial clearance, compensatory 20-HETE pathway effects in some injury contexts, and protective roles for certain DHA-derived diol metabolites (19,20-DHDP) in astrocyte survival. The N-terminal phosphatase domain may have biology distinct from the hydrolase domain. Programs must carefully characterize inhibitor selectivity, dose, duration, and compartment.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Prioritize sEH inhibition as the core AD hypothesis. Focus on lipid-inflammatory and neurovascular mechanisms, especially preservation of 14,15-EET and related EET signaling, rather than generic EPHX2 modulation.
  2. Develop brain-penetrant, hydrolase-selective inhibitors. TPPU and PTUPB are the most directly supported compounds in AD-relevant preclinical material; test in models that capture amyloid, tau, vascular dysfunction, and neuroinflammation simultaneously.
  3. Target AD subgroups most likely to benefit. Patients with vascular cognitive impairment, mixed AD/vascular pathology, hypertension, diabetes/metabolic syndrome, inflammatory signatures, oxidative stress, or cerebrovascular dysfunction represent the highest-priority populations.
  4. Use lipidomic pharmacodynamic biomarkers. EET/DHET ratios — especially 14,15-EET/14,15-DHET balance — should confirm target engagement and stratify therapeutic response in both preclinical studies and clinical trials.
  5. Expand endpoints beyond amyloid and tau. Key measures should include neurovascular coupling, cerebral artery myogenic tone, functional hyperemia, BBB integrity, microglial state, astrocytic lysosomal function, synaptic density, mitochondrial function, and oxidative lipid damage.
  6. Investigate astrocyte- and microglia-specific mechanisms. The strongest cellular hypotheses are that sEH inhibition enhances astrocyte lysosomal amyloid clearance, preserves glutamate support functions (GLT-1), normalizes microglial inflammatory states, and improves microglial tau phagocytosis.
  7. Consider dual sEH/COX-2 inhibition where appropriate. PTUPB-style dual targeting may be especially useful for AD phenotypes dominated by vascular inflammation and prostanoid/oxylipin imbalance; however, safety and isoform specificity must be rigorously evaluated.
  8. Avoid assuming global EPHX2 loss is always beneficial. Distinguish acute versus chronic inhibition, hydrolase versus phosphatase domain effects, brain versus peripheral consequences, and EET preservation versus loss of protective diol metabolites from other PUFA substrates.
  9. Advance human validation before clinical claims. Current evidence is strongest in preclinical models, human genetics, and mechanistic studies. Clinical translation requires demonstrated safety, confirmed brain exposure, long-term dosing studies, cognitive efficacy data, biomarker response, and rigorous subgroup selection.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment —

EPHX2/sEH represents a promising, mechanism-anchored therapeutic target for Alzheimer's disease, supported by convergent preclinical evidence across amyloid, tau, neuroinflammation, cerebrovascular, and oxidative-stress models; human genetic and multi-omics data linking EPHX2 to AD risk and hippocampal integrity; and a growing pharmacological toolkit of named inhibitors. The core therapeutic hypothesis — that inhibiting sEH preserves protective EET lipid mediators, thereby suppressing glial reactivity, improving cerebral perfusion, enhancing amyloid and tau clearance via astrocyte and microglial mechanisms, and protecting synaptic and neuronal integrity — is biologically coherent and multiply supported. Context dependence is the primary scientific caveat: the biology of complete EPHX2 loss differs from selective hydrolase inhibition, peripheral effects differ from brain effects, and some diol metabolites from non-arachidonic PUFA substrates may be beneficial. Clinical validation is absent; the target should be positioned as a disease-modifying candidate requiring brain-penetrant, selective inhibitors, lipidomic biomarker strategies, and enriched patient subgroup designs — particularly those involving vascular cognitive impairment and mixed AD pathology — before claims of therapeutic efficacy can be made in humans.
