Overview

Therapeutic Caution: Human ATP5PO loss-of-function variants cause severe mitochondrial complex V deficiency with neurologic phenotypes. Therapeutic strategies must preserve or normalize ATP5PO/OSCP function — inhibitory approaches are contraindicated.

ATP5PO, also known as OSCP, is a nuclear-encoded regulatory and peripheral stalk subunit of mitochondrial ATP synthase (complex V). Its relevance to Alzheimer's disease (AD) spans mitochondrial bioenergetic failure, oxidative phosphorylation, ATP synthase assembly and function, calcium and oxidative-stress responsiveness, and regulation of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP).

The strongest AD-specific evidence is at the biomarker level: ATP5PO was identified as a female hub gene in a blood transcriptomic AD study and was significantly reduced in both AD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI), suggesting that downregulation may emerge early in disease and may reflect a sex-specific energy-metabolism signature.

Mechanistically, ATP5PO sits at a convergence point for several AD-relevant processes: impaired ATP production, calcium dyshomeostasis, oxidative stress, mitochondrial depolarization, mPTP opening, and age-related decline in mitochondrial stress resilience. These mechanisms align closely with established AD pathogenic themes, though direct therapeutic validation in AD models remains limited.

Key Insights

Core Bioenergetic Role

ATP5PO/OSCP is central to mitochondrial ATP synthase complex V regulation and assembly, making it directly relevant to neuronal energy metabolism and the bioenergetic deficits characteristic of Alzheimer's disease.

Biomarker Potential

ATP5PO was significantly decreased in blood from AD and MCI patients as part of a female-enriched energy-metabolism gene signature. A diagnostic model including ATP5PO and other hub genes achieved an AUC of 0.919 (training) and 0.803 (validation), supporting meaningful biomarker utility.

mPTP & Neuronal Vulnerability

ATP5PO/OSCP regulates mPTP opening during calcium overload and oxidative stress, placing it at the nexus of mitochondrial depolarization and cell death cascades that underlie AD-relevant neuronal vulnerability.

Age-Related Dysfunction

Age-related weakening of OSCP regulatory stability may connect ATP5PO dysfunction to late-life neurodegenerative susceptibility. Predicted TUSC2–OSCP interactions suggest participation in conserved mitochondrial calcium-sensing mechanisms controlling ATP synthase and mPTP behavior.

Post-Translational Regulation

Non-AD mechanistic studies demonstrate that ATP5PO post-translational modifications — including glutathionylation and lactylation — can affect oxidative phosphorylation, membrane potential, mPTP function, and cell survival, broadening the mechanistic toolkit.

Essential for Nervous System

Human ATP5PO loss-of-function variants cause severe mitochondrial complex V deficiency with neurologic phenotypes, confirming that ATP5PO is essential for nervous system bioenergetics and that inhibitory therapeutic strategies are explicitly contraindicated.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — ATP5PO / OSCP in Alzheimer's Disease

ATP5PO/OSCP is a biologically credible and mechanistically well-positioned candidate in Alzheimer's disease research, situated at the intersection of mitochondrial bioenergetics, calcium homeostasis, oxidative stress, and mPTP regulation — all established AD pathogenic themes. Its strongest current evidence is biomarker-level: significant downregulation in blood from both AD and MCI patients, female-enriched hub gene status, and membership in a diagnostic model with strong AUC performance. Mechanistic studies establish that ATP5PO is essential for complex V function and nervous system viability, and that its dysfunction cascades through membrane depolarization, impaired ATP synthesis, and maladaptive mPTP opening into neuronal energy failure. Age-dependent decline in OSCP stability and modulation through post-translational modifications further link it to late-life neurodegenerative vulnerability. However, direct therapeutic validation in AD-specific neuronal and animal models is currently limited, positioning ATP5PO as a high-priority target for further mechanistic and translational investigation — particularly as a mitochondrial-protective rather than amyloid- or tau-directed strategy. Inhibitory approaches must be avoided given the severity of loss-of-function phenotypes in humans.
