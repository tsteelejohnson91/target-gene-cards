MRPL53 is a mitochondrial ribosomal large-subunit protein whose primary role is mitochondrial protein translation. The available literature does not directly link MRPL53 to Alzheimer's disease, amyloid-β, tau pathology, synaptic dysfunction, or neurodegeneration. Its potential relevance to Alzheimer's disease is therefore inferential.

The strongest Alzheimer's-related rationale is that MRPL53 forms part of the core mitoribosomal machinery responsible for synthesizing mitochondrially encoded oxidative phosphorylation proteins. Mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, reduced ATP production, oxidative stress, lipid dysregulation, and metabolic failure are all central themes in Alzheimer's biology — altered MRPL53 expression or function could plausibly contribute to neuronal or glial vulnerability if demonstrated in Alzheimer's-relevant systems.

As a therapeutic target, MRPL53 must be considered hypothesis-generating rather than validated. More plausible approaches would involve restoring abnormal MRPL53 expression, supporting mitoribosome function, improving mitochondrial translation or RNA handling, or targeting upstream stressors that disrupt mitochondrial translation machinery.

Essentiality & Safety Concern

Mitochondrial ribosomal proteins are broadly expressed, essential, and non-redundant. Direct inhibition of MRPL53 could impair mitochondrial protein synthesis and worsen energetic failure — the opposite of a therapeutic goal in Alzheimer's disease.

Cross-Disease Metabolic Context

Overall Assessment

MRPL53 is a structurally and functionally important mitoribosomal protein whose role in oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial translation gives it plausible, conceptual relevance to Alzheimer's disease — a condition in which mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic failure, and bioenergetic collapse are recurring pathological themes. However, no direct evidence connects MRPL53 to Alzheimer's pathology, amyloid, tau, or neurodegeneration. Its appearance in metabolic-stress, cuproptosis, and cancer transcriptional contexts provides useful mechanistic scaffolding but does not substitute for disease-specific validation. The gene's apparent essentiality means that direct pharmacological inhibition carries genuine risk of harm, and any future therapeutic strategy should be oriented toward restoration and stabilization of mitoribosomal function rather than suppression. At this stage, MRPL53 is best positioned as a hypothesis-generating mitochondrial translation node warranting rigorous, Alzheimer's-focused experimental investigation before any translational commitment is made.
