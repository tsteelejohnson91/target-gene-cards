Overview

⚠ Critical Caveat The acronym "GDA" appears throughout the literature in many unrelated contexts — gastroduodenal artery, gene-disease association methods, guideline daily amounts, geldanamycin, glutaraldehyde, and author/institutional abbreviations. These must not be interpreted as molecular evidence for GDA involvement in Alzheimer's disease.

The available evidence linking GDA (guanine deaminase) to Alzheimer's disease is limited and largely indirect. The most relevant direct finding places GDA as a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) protein in an Alzheimer's biomarker proteomics study, where it contributed to distinguishing amyloid/tau-positive (AT+) individuals from amyloid/tau-negative (AT−) controls. A second Alzheimer's-model finding groups GDA among synaptic or neuronal-function markers that are restored following KChIP3 deletion in 5XFAD mice.

Neither finding establishes GDA as causal in Alzheimer's disease. No study in the supplied text tests GDA inhibition, activation, knockdown, overexpression, or pharmacologic targeting in any Alzheimer's model or patient cohort. The text does not show that GDA modulation alters amyloid-β, tau, neuroinflammation, synaptic loss, cognition, or neurodegeneration. GDA is therefore best positioned, on current evidence, as a candidate biomarker or downstream molecular readout rather than a validated therapeutic target.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — GDA in Alzheimer's Disease

On the basis of the supplied evidence, GDA (guanine deaminase) does not meet the criteria for a validated Alzheimer's therapeutic target. The strongest direct findings position GDA as a CSF proteomics biomarker that tracks amyloid/tau positivity, and as a downstream synaptic-integrity readout in the 5XFAD/KChIP3 mouse model. Neither finding demonstrates causality, and no pharmacologic or genetic perturbation of GDA in an AD context is reported. The enzyme's established role in purine/xanthine metabolism is mechanistically interesting given AD-adjacent themes of oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, but those links remain unproven in AD. The dominant therapeutic signal in the text belongs to KChIP3, which influences β-amyloid burden, neuroinflammation, synaptic pruning, and cognitive decline directly. GDA should be monitored as a secondary biomarker candidate and revisited only after targeted causal experiments, CNS localisation studies, and longitudinal clinical data are available.
