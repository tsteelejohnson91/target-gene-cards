Overview

⚠ Safety Concern
CD45 is broadly expressed across all nucleated hematopoietic cells and is essential for T-cell, B-cell, myeloid, and broader immune signaling. Systemic targeting carries substantial risk of immunosuppression, impaired hematopoiesis, reduced antimicrobial defense, and disruption of beneficial inflammatory resolution. Broad CD45 blockade is not recommended as an initial AD strategy.

PTPRC encodes CD45, a receptor-type protein tyrosine phosphatase broadly expressed on leukocytes. Across the supplied evidence, PTPRC/CD45 is most consistently characterized as an immune-cell marker and immune-signaling regulator rather than as a neuron-intrinsic Alzheimer's disease (AD) gene or a classical amyloid/tau target.

The AD-relevant evidence is mixed in strength. Direct associations include: a Mendelian randomization study linking circulating PTPRC biology inversely with AD-related phenotypes (neuroimaging traits mediating ~67% of the relationship); an AD microglial study placing PTPRC in a predicted CD86–PTPRC–ITGAX axis in TIM-3⁺ microglia linked to neuroinflammation and impaired amyloid-β clearance; a CD33–CD45 study suggesting AD-risk-associated full-length CD33 inhibits CD45 phosphatase activity, impairing microglial clearance and worsening amyloid-β-induced dendritic spine loss; a human AD cortex study finding increased Iba1low CD45high myeloid populations correlated with tau pathology; and a GFAP/PTPRC network study linking PTPRC-centered glial/immune network desynchronization with AD progression.

The broader literature consistently places PTPRC within immune activation, T-cell/B-cell signaling, macrophage/microglial activation, cytokine signaling, integrin-mediated adhesion, phagocytosis, neurovascular inflammation, oxidative stress, and interferon/NF-κB/JAK-STAT/TLR pathways — all mechanisms that overlap strongly with AD neuroinflammation, microglial dysfunction, peripheral immune crosstalk, blood–brain barrier disruption, and synaptic vulnerability.

As a therapeutic target, PTPRC/CD45 is best interpreted as a candidate immune-modulatory node rather than a validated AD drug target. Its value would depend on selective modulation of specific CD45-associated pathways, isoforms, ligands, or cell types rather than broad systemic inhibition.

Key Insights

Immune Marker, Not AD-Specific Gene

PTPRC/CD45 is primarily a pan-leukocyte marker and hub of immune-cell activation. The evidence does not support a role as a neuron-intrinsic AD gene or direct regulator of amyloid production or tau phosphorylation.

CD45 May Be Protective in Myeloid Contexts

The CD33–CD45 evidence suggests that AD-risk-associated full-length CD33 inhibits CD45 phosphatase activity, reducing microglial amyloid-β clearance and worsening dendritic spine loss. Preserving CD45 function in CD33-inhibited myeloid cells could therefore be beneficial.

Inflammatory Microglial Axis (TIM-3⁺)

In an AD single-cell/bulk RNA-seq framework, PTPRC was part of a predicted CD86–PTPRC–ITGAX axis in TIM-3⁺ microglia, linked to sustained TNF-α/NF-κB and IL-6/JAK/STAT3 inflammation and impaired amyloid-β clearance through PI3K-AKT and Fcγ receptor pathways.

Tau-Linked Myeloid Dysfunction

In human AD cortex, Iba1low CD45high myeloid populations were increased and correlated with tau pathology rather than amyloid-β load, alongside markers HLA-DR, CD74, CD14, CD33, CD32, and L-Ferritin — suggesting relevance to tau-linked neurodegeneration.

Neuroimaging Mediation (~67%)

A Mendelian randomization study reported that neuroimaging metrics mediated approximately 67% of the inverse relationship between PTPRC and AD-related phenotypic characteristics, suggesting circulating CD45 biology may relate to AD partly through MRI-detectable brain structural changes.

Substantial Safety Concerns

CD45 is essential across all nucleated hematopoietic cells. Systemic targeting could cause immunosuppression, impaired hematopoiesis, altered lymphocyte signaling, reduced antimicrobial defense, and disruption of beneficial inflammatory resolution. Ligand- and axis-specific approaches are far preferable to global blockade.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Prioritize cell-type-resolved validation. Determine whether the PTPRC signal in AD derives from resident microglia, border-associated macrophages, infiltrating monocytes, T cells, B cells, neutrophils, or perivascular immune cells before interpreting pathway directionality.
  2. Test directionality before therapeutic targeting. Establish whether higher CD45 activity is protective or harmful in AD-relevant cells. The CD33–CD45 evidence suggests CD45 function may support amyloid-β clearance, while inflammatory contexts suggest CD45 can also promote immune activation.
  3. Focus on specific immune axes rather than global PTPRC expression. Particularly relevant follow-up axes include CD33–CD45 in myeloid amyloid clearance, CD86–PTPRC–ITGAX in TIM-3⁺ microglia, LGALS9/galectin-mediated CD45 modulation, and CD45-linked integrin/Src-family kinase signaling.
  4. Use PTPRC as part of multi-marker immune signatures. Combine with HLA-DR, CD74, CD14, CD33, CD32, L-Ferritin, ITGB2, ITGAM, TYROBP, TREM2, INPP5D, CSF1R, C1QB, FCGR genes, CCL2/CCR2, TLR2/TLR4, IL1B, TNF, and GFAP to define AD neuroimmune states.
  5. Evaluate PTPRC in relation to tau, not only amyloid. Human AD cortex data linking Iba1low CD45high cells to tau pathology suggests PTPRC-positive myeloid dysfunction may be especially relevant to tau-associated neurodegeneration.
  6. Explore biomarker applications first. PTPRC/CD45 may be useful in blood, CSF, extracellular vesicles, imaging, or single-cell immune profiling to identify patients with immune-active, vascular-inflammatory, or leukocyte-infiltrated AD endotypes.
  7. Avoid broad systemic CD45 blockade as an initial AD strategy. Any therapeutic approach should be cell-type-specific, pathway-specific, transient, localized, or ligand-axis-directed to preserve normal immune function.
  8. Test therapeutic hypotheses with comprehensive functional endpoints. Required endpoints include amyloid burden, tau pathology, synaptic integrity, dendritic spine preservation, microglial phagocytosis, inflammatory cytokines, neurovascular integrity, neuronal survival, and cognition.
  9. Stratify by disease stage and inflammatory subtype. PTPRC-axis modulation may be most relevant in hyper-inflammatory AD subtypes, AD with vascular or blood–brain barrier dysfunction, AD with peripheral immune activation, or mixed AD/vascular dementia phenotypes.
  10. Treat PTPRC as a promising but unvalidated AD target. Current evidence supports PTPRC/CD45 as a biologically plausible immune-modulatory node and biomarker candidate, but not yet as a clinically validated Alzheimer's therapeutic target.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — PTPRC / CD45 in Alzheimer's Disease

PTPRC/CD45 occupies a biologically plausible but therapeutically unvalidated position in AD research. The gene's central role in leukocyte signaling, microglial activation states, amyloid-β clearance biology, and tau-associated myeloid dysfunction places it at the intersection of several core AD pathomechanisms — particularly neuroinflammation, peripheral immune crosstalk, and blood–brain barrier disruption. However, the AD evidence remains predominantly indirect, network-based, or of mixed strength, and PTPRC lacks the direct amyloid/tau-causal evidence that characterizes higher-confidence AD targets. Its greatest near-term value likely lies as a biomarker of immune-active AD endotypes and as a lens for understanding specific myeloid interaction axes — notably CD33–CD45 and CD86–PTPRC–ITGAX. Any therapeutic strategy must contend with the substantial safety risk of impairing normal hematopoietic immune function through broad CD45 targeting, making ligand-axis-directed, cell-type-specific, or transient approaches the only rational path forward if therapeutic development is pursued.
