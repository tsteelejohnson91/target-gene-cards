Overview

Caveat — Limited Direct AD Validation: The strongest disease evidence for SERPINI1 is familial encephalopathy with neuroserpin inclusion bodies (FENIB), not Alzheimer's disease. Direct experimental validation in AD models is currently limited, and SERPINI1 has not been established as a validated AD gene or therapeutic target.

SERPINI1 encodes neuroserpin, a secreted neuronal serine protease inhibitor relevant to Alzheimer's disease through indirect but mechanistically plausible links: regulation of extracellular tPA/plasmin protease activity, neuroprotection, synaptic and neurite biology, proteostasis, ER stress, protein misfolding and polymerization, and shared neurodegenerative transcriptional signatures.

The strongest direct disease evidence for SERPINI1 concerns FENIB — a mutation-driven dementia and epilepsy disorder caused by neuroserpin misfolding, polymerization, intracellular inclusion formation, and neuronal toxicity. This makes SERPINI1 highly relevant to AD-like mechanisms of toxic protein conformation and aggregation, but does not establish it as a validated AD therapeutic target.

Across AD-specific evidence, SERPINI1 appears in broader serpin/neurodegeneration datasets and an AD-COPD comorbidity bioinformatics study, but direct experimental validation in AD models is limited. In one human frontal cortex analysis comparing prion disease with early AD-related pathology, SERPINI1 was dysregulated in sporadic Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, while the stronger AD-associated serpin signal was SERPINA3/SerpinA3n rather than SERPINI1.

Key Insights

tPA/Plasmin Regulation

Neuroserpin selectively inhibits the tPA/plasmin system, implicated in synaptic plasticity, extracellular matrix remodeling, inflammation, excitotoxicity, blood-brain barrier responses, and neurodegenerative injury — all pathways with mechanistic relevance to AD, though direct modulation of AD pathology has not been demonstrated.

FENIB — Aggregation-Driven Dementia

Pathogenic variants (S49P, H338R, G392E, G392R) destabilize neuroserpin, causing polymerization, intracellular inclusions, ER/proteotoxic stress, neuronal death, epilepsy, brain atrophy, and premature death. Disease severity correlates with predicted structural disruption and aggregate burden — a compelling conformational disease model parallel to amyloid-β and tau.

Gene-Editing Therapeutic Proof-of-Concept

Adenine base editing in FENIB cell and iPSC-derived neuronal models corrected a pathogenic SERPINI1 variant, reduced inclusion size and number, and restored neuronal dendritic morphology — supporting precision correction strategies and informing broader anti-aggregation approaches relevant to AD.

Dosage Is Context-Dependent

Zebrafish knockouts showed anxiety-like behavior, motoneuron axon extension defects, and altered neurodegeneration-related gene expression, while overexpression also produced behavioral abnormalities and neurodegeneration-related transcriptomic changes. Therapeutic modulation requires careful calibration — not simple activation or inhibition.

Limited Direct AD Validation

In a human frontal cortex study of prion disease and early AD-related pathology, SERPINI1 was dysregulated in sporadic Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, but the stronger AD-associated serpin signal was SERPINA3/SerpinA3n. No study demonstrates that SERPINI1 modulation improves amyloid burden, tau pathology, synaptic survival, or cognition in AD models.

Bioinformatic Signatures — Exploratory

SERPINI1 was identified as a hub gene in a 2026 AD-COPD shared-gene analysis and as a key gene in a Parkinson's disease cuproptosis-related analysis. It also appears in neuroserpin-deficient mouse CNS proteomic changes spanning apoptosis, glutamate receptor activation, and synaptic signaling. These findings remain computational and require experimental validation.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — SERPINI1 / Neuroserpin in Alzheimer's Disease

SERPINI1 occupies a mechanistically plausible but insufficiently validated position in Alzheimer's disease research. Its strongest disease credentials come from FENIB, where gain-of-toxic-function misfolding and polymerization of neuroserpin recapitulate the conformational disease logic central to AD — a parallel that makes it a scientifically compelling model. Its regulation of the tPA/plasmin extracellular protease system, its associations with synaptic plasticity, neuroprotection, and BBB integrity, and the gene-editing therapeutic proof-of-concept in FENIB neuronal models all support continued mechanistic investigation. However, no published study demonstrates that SERPINI1 modulation directly improves amyloid burden, tau pathology, neuroinflammation, or cognitive outcomes in AD, and stronger AD serpin signals reside in SERPINA3. The dosage-sensitivity observed across zebrafish and overexpression models further cautions against assuming a straightforward therapeutic direction. The most productive near-term path is direct experimental testing in AD models, strategic use of FENIB as a proteostasis platform, and embedding SERPINI1 within multi-gene neurodegenerative signatures rather than pursuing it as a standalone AD target.
