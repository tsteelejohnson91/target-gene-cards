Overview

⚠ Safety Caveat S100A9 contributes to innate host defense and myeloid-cell homeostasis. Chronic systemic inhibition in older AD patients must be evaluated for infection risk, immune suppression, impaired wound repair, and unintended suppression of beneficial microglial clearance activity.

S100A9 is a pro-inflammatory S100-family alarmin that acts principally as the calprotectin / S100A8–A9 heterodimer. Its relevance to Alzheimer's disease spans overlapping mechanisms: amyloid-β handling, tau–S100A9 aggregation interactions, microglial and astrocytic activation, RAGE/TLR4/NF-κB signaling, NLRP3 inflammasome activation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, NETosis, synaptic loss, neuronal injury, and systemic inflammaging.

The strongest AD-specific signals include: direct Ca²⁺-bound S100A9 binding to Aβ40/Aβ42 altering fibrillation; Tau 2N4R enhancing S100A9 aggregation; chronic intranasal S100A9 fibrils impairing spatial memory in aging mice; S100A9 described as a promoter of the inflammatory-amyloid cascade; and Edaravone-Dexborneol directly binding S100A9 in APP/PS1 mice, reducing its expression, phosphorylation, and self-assembly while lowering plaque burden, improving cognition, reducing neuroinflammation/oxidative stress, and enhancing microglial Aβ phagocytosis.

Across broader non-AD studies, S100A9 behaves as an upstream inflammatory driver — not merely a biomarker — amplifying innate immune pathways through RAGE and TLR4, inducing IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, promoting myeloid/neutrophil/macrophage activation, supporting inflammasome/pyroptosis, and driving oxidative and mitochondrial injury. These mechanisms align closely with AD neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative biology.

Key Insights

Direct Amyloid Relevance

Ca²⁺-bound S100A9 binds monomeric Aβ40 and Aβ42 with micromolar affinity, suppressing Aβ40 fibrillation in vitro. This suggests S100A9 may shift Aβ equilibria among monomeric, oligomeric, protofibrillar, and fibrillar forms — with potentially profound consequences for plaque dynamics.

Tau–S100A9 Aggregation Link

Tau 2N4R enhances S100A9 amyloid aggregation and promotes fibril self-association, directly linking a core AD proteinopathy to S100A9 aggregation biology. Chronic intranasal S100A9 fibrils impaired spatial memory in aging mice, establishing fibrillar forms as neurotoxic and cognition-relevant.

AD Mouse Therapeutic Evidence

In APPswe/PS1dE9 mice, Edaravone-Dexborneol directly bound S100A9, inhibiting its expression, threonine phosphorylation, and self-assembly. Treatment improved cognition, reduced Aβ plaque burden, protected synapses, reduced oxidative stress/neuroinflammation, and enhanced microglial Aβ phagocytosis.

Microglial Synapse & Neuron Loss

Extracellular recombinant S100A9 activated microglia, increased inflammatory cytokines, induced synaptic loss at low concentrations, and caused microglia-dependent neuronal loss at higher concentrations — providing a plausible mechanism for synaptic failure and neuronal removal through excessive inflammatory phagocytosis.

RAGE / TLR4 / NF-κB Axis & Inflammasome

S100A9 repeatedly signals through RAGE and TLR4, activating NF-κB, MAPK, and downstream cytokine programs. It is further associated with NLRP3 inflammasome activation, caspase-1, gasdermin D, IL-1β, pyroptosis, and positive-feedback inflammatory loops — all central to AD-associated microglial inflammation.

Safety & Context Dependence

S100A9 contributes to innate host defense and myeloid homeostasis. Complete systemic blockade may impair antimicrobial responses, alter myeloid function, and suppress beneficial microglial clearance. Timing, cell-type specificity, CNS penetration, and inflammatory phenotype selection are likely critical for safe therapeutic targeting.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Prioritize neuroinflammatory subtypes. Target S100A9 as a candidate for AD neuroinflammatory subtypes — not a universal AD driver. The strongest rationale converges on amyloid handling, microglial phagocytosis, RAGE/TLR4/NF-κB signaling, inflammasome activation, oxidative stress, and synaptic injury.
  2. Map S100A9 elevation in AD tissues. Test whether S100A9 is elevated in AD brain regions, CSF, plasma, extracellular vesicles, activated microglia, border-associated macrophages, infiltrating neutrophils/monocytes, or amyloid-associated structures.
  3. Validate causality in AD models. Test whether S100A9 deletion, knockdown, neutralization, or pharmacologic inhibition reduces Aβ burden, tau pathology, microglial NF-κB/NLRP3 activity, cytokine release, synaptic pruning, neuronal loss, and cognitive impairment.
  4. Explore direct therapeutic strategies. Consider S100A9/S100A8–A9 inhibitors, blockade of S100A9–Aβ or Tau–S100A9 interactions, inhibition of S100A9 self-assembly/fibrillization, RAGE or TLR4 receptor blockade, NF-κB/MAPK/NLRP3 pathway inhibition, and targeted delivery to activated microglia or inflammatory myeloid cells.
  5. Use existing leads as translational anchors. Prioritize Edaravone-Dexborneol (AD mice), paquinimod/tasquinimod-like S100A9 inhibitors, ABR-class S100A8/A9 antagonists, neutralizing antibodies, peptide inhibitors of S100A9 aggregation, and RAGE-directed approaches.
  6. Avoid stand-alone biomarker interpretation. S100A9/calprotectin rises in many inflammatory diseases; evaluate it within multi-marker panels that include amyloid, tau, GFAP, NfL, APOE genotype, and vascular-inflammatory measures.
  7. Stratify patients by inflammatory phenotype. S100A9 targeting may be most relevant in S100A9-high, inflammation-high, APOE ε4-associated, vascular-inflammatory, or myeloid/neutrophil-activated AD subgroups.
  8. Design rigorous safety evaluation. Chronic systemic S100A9 inhibition in older AD patients should be evaluated for infection risk, immune suppression, altered wound repair, and unintended effects on beneficial microglial clearance of Aβ.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — S100A9 in Alzheimer's Disease

S100A9 is a biologically plausible and increasingly compelling AD therapeutic candidate, particularly for inflammatory-amyloid and microglial synapse-loss mechanisms. The evidence includes several direct AD-relevant mechanistic findings: Ca²⁺-bound S100A9 binding and modifying Aβ40/Aβ42 fibrillation; Tau 2N4R–driven S100A9 aggregation; cognitive toxicity of S100A9 fibrils in aging mice; and proof-of-concept therapeutic reversal with Edaravone-Dexborneol in APP/PS1 mice. Across broader studies, S100A9 consistently acts as an upstream inflammatory driver through RAGE, TLR4, NF-κB, and NLRP3, rather than a passive biomarker. Microglial S100A9 activation provides a mechanistically coherent path from neuroinflammation to synaptic pruning and neuronal loss. However, AD-specific clinical validation remains limited, S100A9 is not AD-specific as a biomarker alone, and the role of S100A9 in host defense demands careful safety evaluation of any chronic inhibitory strategy. The most productive near-term path is patient stratification by inflammatory phenotype combined with rigorous target validation in AD-specific genetic models.
