Overview

Safety Flag — Oncogenic Risk: HGF/MET signaling is repeatedly associated with tumor survival, invasion, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, metastasis, and drug resistance. Hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) is a pleiotropic growth factor that signals through the MET/c-Met receptor tyrosine kinase. It is broadly implicated in inflammation, angiogenesis, vascular remodeling, tissue repair, regeneration, cell survival, oxidative-stress responses, mitochondrial support, and stem-cell paracrine activity. Downstream signaling cascades include PI3K/AKT, ERK/MAPK, STAT3, β-catenin, NF-κB, and related survival or inflammatory networks.

The evidence connecting HGF to Alzheimer's disease is mixed in strength. Elevated CSF HGF was identified as part of a vascular/inflammatory protein signature associated with preclinical AD and later clinical progression. In a TRIAD cohort QSM study, HGF correlated with immune/inflammatory biomarkers and susceptibility-related tissue changes in AD-relevant brain regions, independent of amyloid-β or tau. The only described AD therapeutic trial — fosgonimeton, a positive modulator of the HGF system — failed to meet primary or secondary clinical endpoints in the LIFT-AD Phase 2/3 trial.

Overall, HGF is not established as a validated AD therapeutic target. It is best characterized as a context-dependent immune-vascular-repair and neurotrophic signaling candidate that may relate to AD through neuroinflammation, neurovascular dysfunction, blood–brain barrier biology, oxidative and mitochondrial stress, and impaired repair mechanisms — rather than through proven direct effects on amyloid-β or tau.

Key Insights

AD Biomarker Evidence — Limited but Direct

Elevated CSF HGF was part of a vascular/inflammatory protein signature including ICAM-1, VCAM-1, neuropilin-1/2, SCARB2, and PLAU, detectable in preclinical AD and predictive of later clinical progression. In the TRIAD cohort, HGF correlated with immune/inflammatory biomarkers and QSM tissue changes in AD-relevant regions, independent of amyloid-β or tau.

Clinical Trial — Fosgonimeton Negative

The only described AD therapeutic test of HGF-system modulation was fosgonimeton (LIFT-AD Phase 2/3) in mild-to-moderate AD. It failed to significantly improve the combined cognition/function endpoint, ADAS-Cog11, ADCS-ADL23, or NfL over 26 weeks. A nominal p-tau217 biomarker signal and small non-significant trends were insufficient to claim efficacy.

Mechanistic Plausibility — Extrapolated, Not AD-Proven

HGF/MET activates PI3K/AKT, ERK/MAPK, STAT3, β-catenin, and NF-κB pathways that overlap with neuronal survival, synaptic resilience, neuroinflammation, insulin/metabolic signaling, tau-relevant kinase biology, autophagy, and oxidative stress. This plausibility is largely inferred from non-AD models and requires direct AD-specific validation.

Neurovascular & Angiogenic Biology

HGF appears alongside VEGF, ANGPT2, FGF, and PDGF in repair and inflammatory settings relevant to AD cerebrovascular dysfunction. Notably, supraphysiological HGF disrupted ZO-1 tight-junction localization, disorganized F-actin, reduced transendothelial electrical resistance, and increased BBB permeability in mouse brain endothelial models — highlighting dose-dependent dual effects.

Exosome & Stem-Cell Paracrine Route

MSCs, dental pulp stem cells, stromal secretomes, and extracellular vesicles repeatedly contain or induce HGF. Non-AD CNS evidence includes HGF-exosomes reducing ischemia/reperfusion injury via c-Met/β-catenin/RNF5-mediated STING degradation, astrocyte-derived Hgf acting on MET-positive cortical neurons during synapse maturation, and HGF/c-Met involvement in spinal cord injury repair. These suggest HGF as one component of a broader trophic cargo rather than an isolated systemic therapy.

Oncogenic Safety — Major Concern

HGF/MET is extensively linked to tumor survival, proliferation, invasion, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, angiogenesis, stromal activation, immune evasion, metastasis, and drug resistance across oncology literature. Chronic systemic HGF activation in older AD populations carries meaningful oncogenic, pro-remodeling, and vascular permeability risks that must be addressed in any clinical strategy.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Treat HGF as an investigational pathway, not an established target. The evidence supports HGF as a plausible immune-vascular-repair and neurotrophic candidate, but not as a proven disease-modifying therapy in AD.
  2. Prioritize biomarker-panel studies over single-marker interpretation. HGF should be studied alongside ICAM-1, VCAM-1, neuropilins, PLAU, IL-6, VEGF, TWEAK, CSF-1, CXCL9, CD40, NfL, GFAP, p-tau217, amyloid markers, and imaging measures such as QSM or vascular MRI.
  3. Clarify whether elevated HGF is protective, compensatory, reactive, or pathogenic. Longitudinal studies should test whether CSF or plasma HGF predicts neuroinflammation, vascular dysfunction, BBB leakage, synaptic loss, cognitive decline, and conversion from preclinical/prodromal AD to dementia.
  4. Test HGF/MET modulation directly in AD models. Required experiments should assess amyloid-β deposition, tau phosphorylation, synaptic density, neuronal survival, microglial and astrocyte phenotypes, BBB integrity, neurovascular coupling, mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, cognition, and regional brain inflammation.
  5. Avoid assuming systemic HGF delivery is appropriate for AD. Brain-targeted, transient, dose-controlled, or cell-specific strategies are likely safer than chronic systemic activation given cancer, angiogenesis, permeability, and proliferative risks.
  6. Consider HGF-enriched exosomes or secretome-based approaches as the more plausible translational route. The text repeatedly links HGF to MSC/DPSC/exosome repair programs, suggesting it works best as one component of a coordinated trophic and immunomodulatory cargo.
  7. Use fosgonimeton results to refine, not dismiss, the target. The LIFT-AD trial indicates positive HGF-system modulation was not clinically effective under the tested conditions. Future approaches would need better patient selection, earlier disease-stage targeting, longer duration, biomarker enrichment, optimized dosing, and stronger CNS target engagement evidence.
  8. Build rigorous safety monitoring into any AD-directed HGF strategy. Trials and preclinical programs should monitor MET activation, angiogenic markers, tumor risk, abnormal proliferation, vascular leakage, fibrosis/remodeling signals, and systemic inflammatory changes.
  9. Distinguish hepatocyte growth factor from other "HGF" abbreviations. Several records in the literature use HGF to denote human gingival fibroblasts, hierarchical Gaussian filter, hereditary gingival fibromatosis, or institutional names — these must be systematically excluded from hepatocyte growth factor conclusions.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — HGF in Alzheimer's Disease

HGF is a biologically plausible, context-sensitive modulator and biomarker of AD-relevant neuroinflammatory and neurovascular repair biology. Its mechanistic links — spanning PI3K/AKT, ERK/MAPK, STAT3, β-catenin, and NF-κB pathways — touch numerous AD-relevant processes including neuronal survival, synaptic maturation, BBB integrity, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial function. Direct AD evidence exists but is limited: CSF HGF participates in an early vascular/inflammatory signature, and HGF correlates with immune-tissue changes in AD-relevant brain regions, yet both independently of canonical amyloid/tau biology. The only AD therapeutic trial — fosgonimeton in LIFT-AD — was negative, failing all primary and secondary clinical endpoints. Major safety concerns from oncology — including tumor invasion, EMT, metastasis, and drug resistance through chronic MET activation — impose significant translational constraints, particularly for older populations. HGF's therapeutic potential in AD remains unresolved and requires targeted AD-specific validation, dose-controlled CNS delivery strategies, comprehensive biomarker-panel co-assessment, and careful safety surveillance before it can be considered a credible disease-modifying candidate.
