Overview

Safety Concern — Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency Type I: Human ITGB2 loss-of-function causes LAD-I, characterised by impaired leukocyte adhesion and migration, recurrent severe infections, poor wound healing, neutrophilia, and potentially life-threatening immune dysfunction. Broad systemic ITGB2 inhibition carries serious risk, particularly in older AD patients.

ITGB2 (integrin subunit beta 2; CD18) is a leukocyte β2-integrin subunit with well-established roles in immune-cell adhesion, migration, phagocytosis, complement-linked signalling, and inflammatory cell–cell communication. In the context of Alzheimer's disease (AD), ITGB2 is not a classical amyloid- or tau-processing gene; its relevance is primarily neuroimmune, vascular-inflammatory, and biomarker-oriented.

The AD-related evidence is heterogeneous. Direct AD associations include ITGB2 in CSF inflammatory and proteomic signatures, AD brain immune and synaptic-pruning networks, hippocampal amyloid-plaque-associated hub proteins, microglia–neuron communication axes, and peripheral blood changes in AD mouse models. A corneal 5×FAD mouse-model study found ITGB2 upregulated alongside ICAM1 and IL-16, coinciding with CD3+ T-cell infiltration and tau hyperphosphorylation. Other entries infer relevance from non-AD inflammatory or CNS-injury contexts. The most defensible synthesis is that ITGB2 is a plausible AD-relevant immune-adhesion and myeloid activation node, but not a validated AD therapeutic target.

Mechanistically, ITGB2 pairs with α-integrin subunits — including ITGAM/CD11b (Mac-1/CR3), ITGAX/CD11c (CR4), and ITGAL/CD11a (LFA-1) — to support leukocyte adhesion to ICAM-family ligands, transendothelial migration, complement-related phagocytosis, cytoskeletal remodelling, and inflammatory signalling. These functions intersect with major AD mechanisms: microglial activation, complement-mediated synaptic pruning, peripheral immune trafficking, blood–brain barrier dysfunction, neurovascular inflammation, phagocytosis of amyloid and synapses, and tau-associated inflammatory injury.

Key Insights

AD Immune & Biomarker Signatures

Direct AD evidence supports ITGB2 as part of immune and inflammatory signatures: increased CSF ITGB2 in AD or AD-risk states, correlation with pTau181 and total tau, inclusion in synaptic-pruning and immune-infiltration hub-gene networks, and association with amyloid-plaque-associated hippocampal protein networks. ITGB2 may track neuroinflammatory remodelling downstream of, or parallel to, core AD pathology.

ICAM1–ITGB2 Adhesion Module

ITGB2's repeated pairing with ICAM1/ICAM2 is mechanistically important. In a 5×FAD corneal model, ITGB2 was upregulated with ICAM1 and IL-16 at 7 months, coinciding with CD3+ T-cell infiltration, tau hyperphosphorylation at Thr181, increased GSK3β activity, and subsequent peripheral nerve loss — pointing to an adhesion/chemoattraction module relevant at blood–brain barrier, neurovascular, meningeal, and ocular barrier tissues.

Mac-1/CR3 & Complement Phagocytosis

ITGB2 pairing with ITGAM/CD11b forms Mac-1/CR3, a complement-related myeloid receptor directly relevant to AD. Complement activation and microglial phagocytosis contribute to synapse loss, plaque responses, and neuroinflammation. ITGB2 may therefore influence both harmful synaptic engulfment and potentially beneficial amyloid and debris clearance — making its role intrinsically dual.

Disease-Associated Microglial Module

Multiple AD-related datasets place ITGB2 near a myeloid/microglial immune module involving TYROBP, TREM2, C1QA/B/C, CSF1R, PTPRC, CD68, TLR2/TLR4, SYK, LCP2, CYBB, and FCER1G. These modules map onto disease-associated microglia, complement signalling, Fc receptor pathways, phagocytosis, oxidative stress, and inflammatory cytokine production.

Safety Liability — LAD-I Risk

ITGB2 has a major safety liability: human loss-of-function causes Leukocyte Adhesion Deficiency type I (LAD-I), with recurrent severe infections, impaired wound healing, neutrophilia, and life-threatening immune dysfunction. Broad systemic inhibition is therefore high-risk, especially in elderly AD patients. Any therapeutic strategy must be cell-state-specific, tissue-targeted, or time-limited.

Therapeutic Evidence & Dual Role

In non-AD inflammatory models (diabetic nephropathy, hepatic ischaemia/reperfusion, TBI, endometriosis), ITGB2 blockade or silencing can reduce leukocyte infiltration, tissue injury, and inflammatory signalling — demonstrating actionable biology. However, suppressing ITGB2 could equally impair beneficial microglial clearance, tissue repair, and host defence. Disease stage, cell type, and ligand partner are likely critical determinants of outcome.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Treat ITGB2 as a candidate neuroimmune biomarker and pathway node, not as a validated AD therapeutic target. The current evidence base is heterogeneous, with direct AD associations predominately in immune and inflammatory signatures rather than in core amyloid or tau biology.
  2. Prioritise validation in AD-specific cellular datasets. Single-cell or spatial transcriptomics of microglia, border-associated macrophages, infiltrating monocytes, neutrophils, T cells, endothelial cells, retina/cornea, and CSF-derived immune markers will be critical to establish cell-type-resolved expression patterns.
  3. Test ITGB2 expression against AD staging and pathological markers, including amyloid burden, pTau181/total tau, synapse loss, complement activation, BBB dysfunction, ICAM1 expression, cognitive decline, and neurovascular pathology, to clarify whether ITGB2 tracks early, mid, or late disease.
  4. Evaluate ITGB2 within multi-marker panels rather than in isolation. Strong candidate companion markers include ICAM1, IL-16, ITGAM, ITGAX, TREM2, TYROBP, C1QA/B/C, C3/C5, GFAP, PTPRC, CD68, TLR2, SYK, IL-1A/IL-1B, and pTau181.
  5. Avoid broad CD18/ITGB2 blockade as a therapeutic strategy. LAD-I risk mandates safer, more targeted approaches: CNS-localised delivery, cell-state-specific modulation, ligand-specific disruption (e.g. ICAM1–ITGB2 at activated neurovascular barriers), or time-limited intervention windows.
  6. Pursue more precise intervention concepts such as disrupting excessive ICAM1–ITGB2 leukocyte adhesion at neurovascular barriers, modulating specific heterodimers (ITGAM/ITGB2 or ITGAX/ITGB2), targeting pathological fibrin–ITGB2 or complement–ITGB2 signalling, or acting downstream via JAK/STAT, NF-κB, ROS/NLRP3, ERK/STAT3, Rac1, or chemokine recruitment pathways.
  7. Design preclinical AD studies to resolve the dual role of ITGB2-positive myeloid states. Key readouts should include amyloid clearance, tau phosphorylation, synapse density, cognitive performance, BBB integrity, immune-cell infiltration, infection susceptibility, and inflammatory cytokine burden — assessed at multiple disease stages.
  8. The strongest near-term application is biomarker development. ITGB2 may help identify AD patients or disease stages with prominent neuroinflammatory, vascular-inflammatory, barrier-associated, ocular, or peripheral immune remodelling components, potentially guiding patient stratification for immunomodulatory trials.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — ITGB2 / CD18 in Alzheimer's Disease

ITGB2 is a biologically plausible, neuroimmune-relevant adhesion and myeloid activation node in AD, but it is not a validated therapeutic target for the disease. Its strongest evidence lies in inflammatory and proteomic biomarker signatures — elevated CSF ITGB2 correlating with pTau181 and tau, membership in disease-associated microglial and complement modules, and upregulation with ICAM1 and IL-16 in a corneal AD mouse model at the stage of T-cell infiltration and tau pathology. Mechanistically, ITGB2-containing heterodimers (Mac-1/CR3, LFA-1, CR4) intersect directly with complement-mediated synaptic pruning, microglial phagocytosis, leukocyte trafficking, and blood–brain barrier dysfunction — all core features of AD neuroinflammation. However, the therapeutic path is constrained by a serious safety liability: human ITGB2 loss-of-function causes LAD-I, and broad inhibition is incompatible with the clinical needs of elderly AD patients. Furthermore, ITGB2's role is inherently dual — it supports both harmful immune recruitment and potentially beneficial microglial clearance. The most actionable near-term research priority is to develop ITGB2 as a component of multi-marker neuroinflammatory biomarker panels, while any long-term therapeutic hypothesis must focus on selective, cell-state-specific, and spatially restricted modulation validated directly in AD-relevant models.
