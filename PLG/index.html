PLG / Plasminogen & Alzheimer's Disease

Target Enabling Package — AI-Assisted Evidence Summary

Overview

Caution — Dual-edged biology: Broad PLG/plasmin activation may increase hemorrhage risk, blood–brain barrier disruption, extracellular matrix degradation, and pathogen-assisted CNS invasion. Any therapeutic strategy must weigh fibrinolytic benefit against these hazards, particularly in patients with cerebral amyloid angiopathy or compromised vascular integrity.

PLG in the Alzheimer's-relevant literature refers to plasminogen, the zymogen precursor of plasmin. Multiple unrelated uses of the abbreviation "PLG" — including poly(lactide-co-glycolide), poly-L-glutamic acid, and piperlonguminine — appear in the broader corpus but provide no evidence for plasminogen biology in Alzheimer's disease and should not be conflated.

The available literature contains little to no direct causal evidence linking PLG to Alzheimer's disease modification. The strongest AD-specific signals are associative: PLG was identified as a candidate blood gene-expression feature in an ADNI machine-learning study, and HDL-associated PLG was linked to lower temporoparietal brain volume in older adults from the ARIC cohort. These findings position PLG as a candidate biomarker or mechanistic clue rather than a validated therapeutic target.

Mechanistically, PLG is repeatedly connected to fibrinolysis, coagulation, complement biology, neuroinflammation, blood–brain barrier dysfunction, extracellular matrix remodeling, and immune-cell regulation. These processes overlap substantially with non-neuronal mechanisms implicated in Alzheimer's disease — particularly neurovascular injury, fibrin deposition, chronic neuroinflammation, impaired clearance, and complement/coagulation activation.

Key Insights

Biologically Plausible, Not Validated

PLG/plasminogen sits at the intersection of fibrin clearance, proteolysis, inflammation, complement/coagulation cascades, vascular injury, and tissue remodeling — all processes relevant to AD. However, no direct causal or therapeutic evidence in Alzheimer's disease has been established in the supplied literature.

Associative AD Evidence Only

An ADNI blood transcriptomic machine-learning study identified PLG among novel candidate genes for AD risk/staging, with moderate performance and no established causality. An ARIC HDL proteomics study linked higher HDL-associated PLG to lower temporoparietal brain volume, suggesting structural vulnerability rather than amyloid burden.

Activity Matters More Than Abundance

Hereditary PLG deficiency and dysplasminogenemia demonstrate that patients can have normal antigen levels but markedly reduced PLG activity. AD biomarker studies should therefore measure functional plasmin-generating capacity, proteoforms, activators, inhibitors (PAI-1/SERPINE1, α2-antiplasmin), and fibrinolytic outputs rather than total PLG abundance alone.

Fibrinolysis as the Strongest Mechanistic Bridge

PLG variants and reduced activity associate with impaired fibrinolysis and cerebral infarction; PLG appears in stroke/TIA biomarker analyses; PLG deficiency causes fibrin-rich lesions. In AD, fibrin deposition, microvascular disease, BBB leakage, microinfarcts, and vascular inflammation may together accelerate neurodegeneration through this pathway.

Inflammatory Resolution & Efferocytosis

Plasminogen/plasmin increased specialized pro-resolving mediators (lipoxin A4, maresin 1, resolvin D1) in macrophages and enhanced efferocytosis of apoptotic neutrophils via a 5-lipoxygenase-dependent mechanism. By analogy, this may be relevant to microglial debris clearance and protein aggregate removal in AD — though not yet tested in AD brain.

Dual-Edged Safety Profile

Pathogens exploit host PLG to cross CNS barriers — Streptococcus suis enolase-bound PLG enhanced traversal across human cerebral microvascular endothelial monolayers after plasmin conversion. Broad PLG/plasmin activation may also increase hemorrhage risk, ECM degradation, and barrier disruption, especially relevant in patients with cerebral amyloid angiopathy or pre-existing vascular compromise.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Treat PLG as a hypothesis-generating target, not a validated one. The evidence supports mechanistic plausibility through vascular-inflammatory pathways but provides no direct disease-modifying evidence in Alzheimer's disease. All downstream decisions should be governed accordingly.
  2. Prioritize functional assays over abundance-only measurements. Future AD studies should assess PLG activity, plasmin generation, tPA/uPA activity, PAI-1/SERPINE1 inhibition, α2-antiplasmin levels, PLG proteoforms, fibrin burden, D-dimer/fibrinolysis markers, and compartment-specific concentrations in plasma, CSF, and brain vasculature.
  3. Focus on AD subtypes with vascular or inflammatory features. The best-fit patient subgroups likely include individuals with cerebral small-vessel disease, BBB dysfunction, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, high fibrin/coagulation signatures, microinfarcts, elevated complement activation, or strong neuroinflammatory profiles.
  4. Test directionality in AD models before considering intervention. Studies should determine whether increasing PLG/plasmin activity improves amyloid/fibrin clearance and inflammation resolution, or whether it worsens BBB leakage, ECM degradation, bleeding risk, or neuroinflammatory injury in the AD context.
  5. Examine PLG within APOE, APP, complement, and fibrinogen networks. PLG's co-occurrence with APOE, APP, C4A/C5, FGA/FGB/FGG, SERPINE1, and coagulation/complement factors suggests that network-level analysis is more informative than evaluating PLG in isolation.
  6. Consider localized rather than systemic modulation. Because broad PLG/plasmin activation may increase hemorrhage or barrier-disruption risk, any therapeutic approach should favor spatially restricted activation, CNS-targeted delivery, receptor-guided modulation (e.g., via Plg-RKT), or selective blockade of pathological PLG-binding interactions.
  7. Separate plasminogen biology from unrelated PLG abbreviations. Poly(lactide-co-glycolide) and poly-L-glutamic acid studies may be relevant to drug delivery or nanomedicine, but they provide no evidence for plasminogen as an AD mechanism or target and must not be conflated.
  8. Commission direct AD-focused validation studies. Required work includes longitudinal plasma/CSF/brain PLG activity profiling; correlation with amyloid, tau, neurodegeneration, cognition, BBB markers, and vascular imaging; mechanistic testing in AD animal and human-cell models; and safety studies focused on hemorrhage, BBB integrity, inflammatory consequences, and protease balance.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — PLG / Plasminogen in Alzheimer's Disease

PLG/plasminogen is a biologically plausible but unvalidated candidate in Alzheimer's disease research. The strongest available evidence is associative — candidate gene-expression signals from ADNI machine learning and an HDL proteomics association with reduced temporoparietal volume from ARIC — rather than causal or interventional. Mechanistically, plasminogen's central roles in fibrin clearance, vascular integrity, complement/coagulation network regulation, and inflammatory resolution place it at multiple points of convergence with established AD pathophysiology. The fibrinolysis-BBB-neuroinflammation axis represents the most credible bridge between PLG biology and neurodegeneration. Critically, PLG activity rather than abundance is the relevant functional variable, and both deficiency (fibrin-rich lesions) and excess (barrier disruption, pathogen exploitation, bleeding risk) carry harm potential. Therapeutic direction remains genuinely ambiguous: enhancement of fibrinolytic and pro-resolving activity could be beneficial in vascular-inflammatory AD subtypes, while indiscriminate activation poses serious safety risks. The priority next steps are functional biomarker profiling in AD cohorts, network-level co-analysis with APOE/fibrinogen/complement factors, and rigorous directional testing in AD-relevant preclinical models before any intervention strategy is pursued.
