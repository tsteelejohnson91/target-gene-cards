Overview

Validation Caveat: PREPL is a mechanistically plausible but incompletely validated AD target. Clinical and robust in vivo Alzheimer's model validation is currently insufficient for therapeutic translation; findings to date are largely from postmortem multiomics, cell-based knockdown studies, and non-AD disease contexts.

PREPL is best characterized as a mechanistically plausible but still incompletely validated Alzheimer's disease–relevant protein. The strongest PREPL-specific AD evidence comes from human postmortem multiomics and network analyses reporting PREPL downregulation in sporadic late-onset AD brains, with functional PREPL knockdown producing AD-relevant cellular changes: disrupted protein trafficking and vesicle biology, altered neuropeptide secretion, increased secreted Aβ42, increased tau phosphorylation, altered synaptic protein levels, and impaired hippocampal long-term potentiation in mice.

Beyond the AD-focused data, PREPL is repeatedly linked to mitochondrial function, trans-Golgi/AP1-mediated trafficking, synaptic and neuromuscular physiology, neurodevelopment, cytoskeletal organization, and metabolic regulation. These processes overlap substantially with core AD mechanisms including neuronal energy failure, vesicle and endosomal dysfunction, synaptic decline, altered amyloid and tau handling, and vascular-metabolic stress.

PREPL should be distinguished from PREP/POP/PEP — related prolyl oligopeptidase and prolyl endopeptidase enzymes that are more extensively studied as CNS and neurodegeneration targets. POP/PREP inhibitor literature supports the broader druggability of related prolyl-processing enzymes, but does not directly validate PREPL inhibition or activation for AD. PREPL's own biology appears more complex than simple peptidase inhibition, with both enzymatic and nonenzymatic functions likely contributing to disease-relevant pathways.

Key Insights

Direct AD Signal

PREPL was reported as downregulated in sporadic late-onset AD brain via integrative postmortem multiomics and network analysis. PREPL knockdown altered AD-relevant pathways rather than acting merely as a passive marker, producing increases in secreted Aβ42 and tau phosphorylation — the most disease-specific PREPL evidence available.

Synaptic Plasticity & LTP

PREPL reduction changed multiple synaptic protein levels and impaired hippocampal long-term potentiation in mice, directly linking PREPL loss to synaptic dysfunction — a major cellular correlate of cognitive decline in Alzheimer's disease.

Mitochondrial Mechanisms

PREPL deficiency reduces mitochondrial complex activities, disrupts mitochondrial gene expression and ultrastructure, and affects Complex IV / cytochrome c oxidase biogenesis. Coenzyme Q10 and PREPL overexpression have rescued mitochondrial dysfunction in non-AD disease contexts, offering a translatable rescue strategy.

Vesicle Trafficking & Secretion

PREPL knockdown disrupts vesicle structure, neuropeptide-processing enzyme levels, and neuropeptide secretion. Independent work links PREPL to AP1-mediated trans-Golgi network transport partly through nonenzymatic functions — highly relevant because endosomal, Golgi, secretory, and vesicular trafficking defects are central to AD pathobiology.

Enzymatic vs. Nonenzymatic Complexity

PREPL has both catalytic and noncatalytic roles. Some disease-associated missense variants preserve hydrolase activity while disrupting protein–protein interactions. Hydrolase activity is required for normal mitochondrial function, while AP1/trans-Golgi transport regulation can occur independently — drug discovery must address both modes.

POP/PREP: Supportive, Not Interchangeable

Related POP/PREP enzymes have inhibitor programs affecting neuropeptide processing, neuroinflammation, autophagy, and α-synuclein aggregation, demonstrating broader family druggability. However, these data should not be treated as direct PREPL evidence — PREPL-specific assays and selective modulators are required.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Treat PREPL as an exploratory, not validated, therapeutic target. The evidence is promising — PREPL downregulation and knockdown affect Aβ42, tau phosphorylation, trafficking, synaptic proteins, LTP, and mitochondria — but robust clinical and in vivo AD validation remains insufficient for therapeutic translation.
  2. Prioritize validation in AD-relevant systems. Key experiments should measure PREPL expression, localization, hydrolase activity, and interaction networks across AD stages, brain regions, and cell types, especially hippocampal and cortical neurons, astrocytes, microglia, and endothelial/vascular cells.
  3. Test causal effects in AD models. PREPL knockdown, overexpression, rescue, and catalytic-dead/noncatalytic mutant studies should be performed in amyloid, tau, synaptic, and human iPSC-derived neuronal and glial models to determine effects on Aβ42 secretion, APP trafficking, tau phosphorylation, synaptic vesicle biology, mitochondrial function, and neuronal survival.
  4. Separate catalytic from noncatalytic mechanisms. Drug discovery should not assume that active-site inhibition is beneficial. PREPL-directed therapy may need to preserve or enhance mitochondrial-supporting enzymatic activity while restoring protein–protein interaction and AP1/trans-Golgi trafficking functions.
  5. Identify endogenous PREPL substrates and binding partners. This is essential for rational therapeutic design, biomarker development, and safety prediction. Particular attention should be given to lipid-modified/thioester-linked substrates, mitochondrial lipid/protein synthesis pathways, vesicle-trafficking proteins, and synaptic secretion machinery.
  6. Explore restoration strategies. Given PREPL downregulation in AD and harmful effects of PREPL reduction, plausible interventions include enhancing PREPL expression, stabilizing PREPL protein, correcting PREPL-dependent trafficking defects, supporting mitochondrial downstream pathways, or targeting disease-relevant PREPL interaction networks.
  7. Investigate mitochondrial rescue approaches. Coenzyme Q10 rescued mitochondrial dysfunction in PREPL-deficient cells in non-AD contexts, and PREPL overexpression rescued mitochondrial dysfunction in patient fibroblasts. Similar rescue approaches should be tested in AD-relevant neuronal and glial models.
  8. Avoid conflating PREPL with PREP/POP. POP/PREP inhibitors may inform chemical tractability and family-level neurodegeneration mechanisms, but they do not establish how PREPL should be targeted. PREPL-specific assays and selective modulators are required for meaningful progress.
  9. Build biomarker evidence. PREPL protein level, localization, enzymatic activity, and downstream mitochondrial/trafficking signatures should be evaluated as potential biomarkers of AD subtype, disease stage, synaptic vulnerability, or metabolic dysfunction.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — PREPL in Alzheimer's Disease

PREPL sits at a mechanistic intersection highly relevant to Alzheimer's disease — encompassing mitochondrial homeostasis, vesicle and protein trafficking, synaptic plasticity, amyloid and tau-associated changes, and metabolic regulation. The direct AD evidence — postmortem downregulation in sporadic late-onset AD brain and functional knockdown effects on Aβ42 secretion, tau phosphorylation, synaptic proteins, and hippocampal LTP — is scientifically compelling and distinguishes PREPL from a purely circumstantial association. However, PREPL remains an early-stage, exploratory target: in vivo AD model efficacy, selective chemical tools, defined substrates, and clinical correlation data are all currently insufficient. The most plausible therapeutic direction is restoration or enhancement of PREPL-dependent functions rather than broad inhibition, but this requires substantial validation before clinical translation can be contemplated. PREPL warrants continued, carefully staged investigation as a novel node in the AD pathomechanistic landscape.
