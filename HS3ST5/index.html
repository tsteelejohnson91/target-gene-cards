Overview

⚠ Interpretive Caution The AD-associated GWAS locus spans HS3ST5, HDAC2, and MARCKS; causality cannot currently be assigned to HS3ST5 alone. Therapeutic modulation is premature without functional disambiguation of these co-localized candidates.

HS3ST5 encodes heparan sulfate-glucosamine 3-O-sulfotransferase 5, an enzyme that modifies heparan sulfate (HS) proteoglycans by adding 3-O-sulfate groups. Its relevance to Alzheimer's disease (AD) is plausible but not yet definitive. The strongest AD-related evidence derives from a 2023 cross-disorder GWAS identifying a shared ADRD–ALS risk locus near the HS3ST5/HDAC2/MARCKS gene cluster, placing HS3ST5 within a neurodegeneration-associated genomic region without proving causal gene identity.

Mechanistically, HS3ST5 could contribute to AD through heparan sulfate proteoglycan biology — regulating extracellular signaling, ligand binding, cell–matrix interactions, growth factor availability, neuroinflammation, synaptic maintenance, and potentially protein aggregation or clearance. However, the available evidence does not directly demonstrate that HS3ST5 modifies amyloid-β, tau, APOE biology, microglial activation, or cognition in AD models.

Overall, HS3ST5 is best viewed as a candidate modifier of neurodegenerative vulnerability rather than a validated Alzheimer's therapeutic target.

Key Insights

Genetic Signal — GWAS Locus

The primary AD-relevant evidence is indirect: HS3ST5 sits within a shared ADRD–ALS risk locus alongside HDAC2 and MARCKS, identified in a 2023 cross-disorder GWAS. The signal implicates neurodegeneration broadly but does not assign causality to HS3ST5 specifically.

Locus Ambiguity — Alternative Candidates

HDAC2 and MARCKS are strong competing candidates within the same genomic region. Either or both may explain some or all of the observed neurodegenerative association. Therapeutic targeting of HS3ST5 requires functional disambiguation of these co-localized genes.

Heparan Sulfate Biology in Neurodegeneration

HS3ST5 generates specific 3-O-sulfated heparan sulfate structures including the HS4C3 epitope. Altered HS3ST5 activity could change protein–glycan interactions in neural tissue, with plausible downstream effects on extracellular signaling, synaptic maintenance, neuroinflammation, and protein aggregation or clearance — all processes relevant to AD.

Neurodevelopmental & Brain Phenotype Links

HS3ST5 appears repeatedly in neurodevelopmental and brain-phenotype studies: developmental delay, intellectual disability, microcephaly, cerebellar and pontine abnormalities, gray matter volume variation, and cognition-related traits — supporting a broader role in neural tissue integrity.

Hippocampal Stress & DNA Damage

A radiation-induced hippocampal DNA damage study found altered Hs3st5 expression, indirectly linking the gene to hippocampal stress responses and DNA damage pathways relevant to neurodegeneration. This finding is associative, not mechanistically characterized.

Vascular Risk — Indirect Dementia Relevance

Human genetic variation in HS3ST5 is associated with salt-sensitive blood pressure responses, placing it within the broader glycosaminoglycan regulatory system and vascular biology. This may be indirectly relevant to dementia risk, but no direct AD link has been established from this signal.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — HS3ST5 is a plausible but unvalidated candidate in Alzheimer's disease and related neurodegeneration. Its strongest AD-linked evidence is a shared ADRD–ALS GWAS locus, but this signal is confounded by the proximity of HDAC2 and MARCKS, two neurobiologically potent genes in the same region. Mechanistically, HS3ST5's enzymatic role in generating specific 3-O-sulfated heparan sulfate structures provides a credible route to influence extracellular signaling, neuroinflammation, and synaptic biology — all processes germane to AD — but no direct experimental evidence yet links HS3ST5 activity to amyloid-β, tau, or microglial pathology. Supporting observations from neurodevelopmental phenotypes, hippocampal stress responses, and vascular blood pressure genetics add circumstantial plausibility. The highest-priority next steps are locus fine-mapping, causal gene attribution, and functional perturbation experiments in AD-relevant systems, before any therapeutic modulation strategy can be responsibly pursued.
