Overview

⚠ Therapeutic Caution: DLG2 manipulation carries bidirectional risk — enhancing it could worsen hyperexcitability or excitotoxicity, while suppressing it may impair LTP, synaptic stability, and cognition. Any intervention requires region-, stage-, isoform-, and interaction-specificity.

DLG2, also known as PSD-93/chapsyn-110, is an Alzheimer's disease–relevant postsynaptic scaffolding molecule rather than a classical amyloid- or tau-production gene. Its strongest connections to AD center on synaptic failure, glutamatergic/NMDA receptor signaling, hippocampal and cortical circuit dysfunction, excitotoxic stress, oxidative injury, neuroinflammation, and cognitive impairment.

Direct AD-related evidence spans rare-variant and polygenic genetic associations, postmortem AD brain expression and proteomic findings, AD mouse-model studies, and AD-relevant intervention studies. DLG2 appears as a candidate AD risk gene in rare-variant whole-genome sequencing and synapse-restricted polygenic risk analyses; as a hub gene in AD postmortem miRNA/mRNA and sex-specific glial transcriptomic networks; as a hippocampal protein with an early-up, late-down pattern across Braak stages; and as a synaptic protein reduced in APP/PS1 mice but restored functionally by PSD-93 overexpression.

Mechanistically, DLG2 encodes a MAGUK-family postsynaptic density scaffold that organizes receptor and signaling complexes — especially NMDA receptor–associated complexes involving NR2A/NR2B, Fyn, SynGAP, nNOS, potassium channels, and related PSD-family proteins such as PSD-95/DLG4 and SAP102/DLG3. These functions align closely with core AD mechanisms because synaptic dysfunction and impaired plasticity are among the earliest and strongest correlates of cognitive decline.

DLG2's therapeutic potential is plausible but not yet validated clinically. The current evidence supports it best as a synaptic resilience marker, pathway node, or modulator of disease-relevant protein interactions rather than as a straightforward target for global activation or inhibition.

Key Insights

Synaptic Scaffold Biology

DLG2 supports postsynaptic density organization, glutamatergic synapse stability, AMPA/NMDA receptor-associated signaling, dendritic spine function, long-term potentiation, and hippocampal/cortical circuit performance — placing it at the intersection of core AD synaptic mechanisms.

Preclinical Protective Evidence

In APP/PS1 mice, hippocampal PSD-93 was decreased; lentiviral overexpression improved spatial memory, facilitated LTP, reduced plaque burden, and lowered Aβ levels through increased SSTR4 and neprilysin, suggesting enhanced Aβ catabolism as a protective mechanism.

Human Proteomics: Stage-Dependent

Hippocampal CA1/subiculum proteomics across Braak stages revealed an "early-up, late-down" DLG2 pattern consistent with early compensatory synaptic remodeling followed by late synaptic collapse — a pattern shared with other synaptic proteins.

Genetic Nomination

Rare-variant whole-genome sequencing associated a DLG2 region with AD, and a synapse-restricted polygenic risk score for late-onset AD included DLG2 among novel loci within pathways involving glutamate signaling, APP/Aβ biology, and tau binding.

Tau-Related Synaptic Toxicity

In a P301S human tau model, mutant tau reduced PSD-93/DLG2 and synapsin 1 via KEAP1-mediated suppression of NRF2/ARE signaling. Restoring NRF2 activity rescued synaptic protein expression and behavioral deficits, implicating DLG2 loss in tauopathy-driven synapse failure.

Bidirectional Therapeutic Risk

DLG2 downregulation marks synaptic deterioration in denervation, tauopathy, and amyloid models. However, excessive scaffolding could participate in hyperexcitability or excitotoxic signaling. Broad modulation is therefore risky; isoform- and interaction-specific approaches are required.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Treat DLG2 as a synaptic resilience node, not a validated drug target. Current evidence supports DLG2 as a candidate pathway node and marker rather than a straightforward target for global activation or inhibition in AD.
  2. Prioritize AD-specific validation in human tissue and models. Determine whether DLG2 changes occur early, vary by Braak stage, differ by brain region or cell type, and correlate with synapse loss, cognition, amyloid, tau, and neuroinflammation.
  3. Investigate isoform- and interaction-specific biology. Identify which PSD-93 isoforms and domains are altered in AD and which protein interactions are protective versus harmful, rather than treating all DLG2 activity uniformly.
  4. Explore pathway-level therapeutic strategies. Promising mechanisms include preserving postsynaptic scaffold integrity, stabilizing glutamatergic synapses, restoring NMDA receptor signaling balance, protecting dendritic spines, and preventing maladaptive remodeling after entorhinal–hippocampal disconnection.
  5. Test the KEAP1–NRF2/ARE axis as an upstream rescue pathway. NRF2 activation rescued tau-induced reductions in DLG2/PSD-93 and other synaptic proteins; antioxidant and anti-excitotoxic strategies may similarly preserve DLG2-linked synaptic architecture.
  6. Evaluate selective protein–protein interaction targeting. Focus on specific PSD-93 interactions — such as NMDA receptor/Fyn/nNOS/SynGAP-linked toxic signaling or SSTR4/neprilysin-linked Aβ catabolism — rather than modulating total DLG2 levels.
  7. Consider nonpharmacologic and neuromodulatory approaches. Exercise, direct current stimulation, and antioxidant-like interventions were associated with modulation or preservation of DLG2-containing synaptic networks in AD-relevant or aging/injury models.
  8. Use DLG2 as a potential biomarker or stratification feature. Rare variants, synaptic PRS contribution, expression/protein-stage patterns, astrocyte network membership, and imaging-genetics associations may help identify AD subgroups with prominent synaptic, glutamatergic, or circuit-resilience mechanisms.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — DLG2 / PSD-93 in Alzheimer's Disease DLG2/PSD-93 is an AD-relevant postsynaptic scaffolding protein connected to early synaptic dysfunction, hippocampal vulnerability, glutamatergic signaling failure, tau/Aβ-associated synaptotoxicity, and cognitive decline. Its evidence base spans genetic nomination (rare variants and synapse-restricted PRS), human postmortem proteomics (Braak-stage-dependent expression), preclinical rescue experiments (lentiviral PSD-93 overexpression in APP/PS1 mice), and tau-model mechanistic insight (KEAP1–NRF2/ARE pathway). Despite this breadth, DLG2 is not yet a validated standalone drug target: its large scaffold architecture, multiple isoforms, MAGUK-family redundancy, and bidirectional risk profile make global modulation potentially harmful. Therapeutic potential is most credibly pursued through upstream pathway rescue (NRF2/antioxidant approaches), selective protein–protein interaction targeting (NMDA-R/Fyn/nNOS or SSTR4/neprilysin nodes), and use of DLG2 as a synaptic biomarker for patient stratification in trials emphasizing synaptic or glutamatergic mechanisms.
