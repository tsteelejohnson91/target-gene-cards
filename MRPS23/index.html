Overview

⚠ Therapeutic Caution: MRPS23 is required for mitochondrial translation and OXPHOS. Inherited deficiency causes mitochondrial disease with neurological manifestations. In post-mitotic neurons, suppression could worsen bioenergetic failure rather than alleviate it. No inhibitory therapeutic strategy is currently supported.

MRPS23 is a nuclear-encoded mitochondrial ribosomal small-subunit protein required for mitochondrial translation and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). The available evidence provides no direct evidence that MRPS23 is an established Alzheimer's disease (AD) gene, biomarker, or therapeutic target. Its relevance to AD is mechanistic and inferential, mainly through pathways central to AD biology: mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired respiratory-chain activity, oxidative stress, neuronal energy failure, immune-inflammatory signaling, and proteostasis/kinase-related regulation.

The strongest AD-relevant connection is mitochondrial bioenergetics. MRPS23 disruption can destabilize mitochondrial ribosome function, impair mitochondrial protein translation, and cause combined respiratory-chain defects, especially involving complexes I and IV — defects that overlap with core AD mechanisms.

MRPS23-related disease evidence is neurologically relevant but not AD-specific. Bi-allelic MRPS23 variants have been associated with mitochondrial disorders and adult neurological syndromes involving ataxia, neuropathy, pyramidal features, leukoencephalopathy, elevated CSF protein, and profound cerebral folate deficiency. Notably, a folinic-acid response in adult MRPS-related leukoencephalopathy suggests that some downstream biochemical consequences may be therapeutically approachable.

Key Insights

Mitochondrial Translation — Primary AD Link

MRPS23 is needed for mitoribosomal integrity and translation of mitochondrial-encoded OXPHOS components. Deficiency or damaging variants impair respiratory-chain function and energy production — processes highly relevant to metabolically vulnerable neurons in AD.

Complex I & IV Defects Overlap AD Biology

MRPS23 disruption can cause combined respiratory-chain defects, especially Complexes I and IV. These exact defects are repeatedly implicated in AD, where reduced mitochondrial respiration, oxidative stress, and synaptic energy failure drive neuronal loss.

Neurological Disease — Biological Plausibility

Adult-onset MRPS-related disorders show impaired mitochondrial translation, multiple OXPHOS defects, white-matter pathology, and CSF biochemical abnormalities. This demonstrates MRPS23-related mitochondrial impairment can affect the adult brain, though not specifically in an AD pattern.

Oxidative Stress as a Bridge Mechanism

Altered MRPS23 dosage or stability has been linked to OXPHOS changes and mitochondrial ROS in non-AD contexts. Because mitochondrial ROS contributes to synaptic injury, tau/Aβ toxicity, and neuronal vulnerability, MRPS23 dysfunction could theoretically influence AD-relevant oxidative stress.

Cancer-Derived Signaling: Wnt, PI3K-AKT

MRPS23 has been linked to Wnt/β-catenin signaling, proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and treatment resistance in cancer, and is regulated by phosphorylation and methylation affecting PI3K-AKT and survival programs. Wnt is relevant to AD synapses and neuroinflammation, but cancer-based mechanisms cannot be assumed to apply to neurons.

Not a Validated AD Target — Inhibition Risk

Simple inhibition would likely be risky: MRPS23 is required for mitochondrial translation, and inherited deficiency causes mitochondrial disease with neurological manifestations. In post-mitotic AD neurons, suppressing MRPS23 could worsen bioenergetic failure rather than treat it.

Actionable Takeaways

  1. Treat MRPS23 as a candidate mechanism node, not an established target. Its most defensible relevance is through mitoribosomal integrity, mitochondrial translation, OXPHOS, ROS production, and metabolic stress — not through direct evidence in AD pathogenesis.
  2. Prioritize validation in AD-relevant human data. Key studies should assess MRPS23 mRNA, protein abundance, localization, and post-translational modifications in AD brain regions and cell types, including neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, and vascular cells.
  3. Test association with AD pathology and clinical phenotypes. MRPS23 measures should be correlated with amyloid burden, tau pathology, synaptic loss, mitochondrial respiratory markers, neuroinflammation, white-matter changes, cognitive decline, and disease stage.
  4. Use functional models before considering therapeutic modulation. MRPS23 knockdown, overexpression, stabilization, or rescue should be tested in AD-relevant neuronal, astrocyte, microglial, organoid, and animal models to determine whether modulation improves or worsens mitochondrial function, ROS, synaptic resilience, inflammatory activation, amyloid/tau toxicity, and neurodegeneration.
  5. Avoid assuming inhibition is therapeutic. Unlike cancer, where MRPS23 suppression may reduce proliferation, AD neurons are post-mitotic and dependent on mitochondrial function. Any future therapeutic strategy would more likely involve restoring MRPS23 function, stabilizing mitoribosome integrity, or correcting downstream metabolic consequences.
  6. Consider downstream metabolic support as a hypothesis. The folinic-acid response in adult MRPS-related leukoencephalopathy suggests that some MRPS-associated neurological phenotypes may be modifiable through metabolic intervention — providing a cautious rationale for exploring downstream biochemical support rather than direct MRPS23 inhibition.

Overall Assessment

Overall Assessment — MRPS23 & Alzheimer's Disease

MRPS23 represents a biologically plausible but inferential link to Alzheimer's disease. Its role as a nuclear-encoded mitoribosomal subunit places it at the center of mitochondrial translation and OXPHOS regulation — processes that are unambiguously impaired in AD neurons. The strongest mechanistic bridges are Complex I and IV respiratory-chain defects, elevated mitochondrial ROS, and synaptic bioenergetic failure, all of which overlap with core AD pathophysiology. Adult-onset neurological disease caused by bi-allelic MRPS23 variants provides human proof-of-concept that MRPS23 dysfunction can injure the mature nervous system, and the folinic-acid response in MRPS-related leukoencephalopathy opens a speculative door to metabolic intervention. However, there is currently no direct evidence of MRPS23 alteration in AD brain, no causal AD model data, and no cell-type-specific mechanistic understanding. Critically, inhibition is contraindicated as a therapeutic hypothesis: a future strategy, if evidence accrues, should focus on restoring or stabilizing MRPS23 function. At present, MRPS23 should be classified as an early-stage, hypothesis-generating candidate warranting systematic investigation in AD-relevant human tissue and functional models before any translational investment.
